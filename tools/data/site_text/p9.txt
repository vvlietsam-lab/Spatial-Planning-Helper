    "h": "What the article asks and why",
    "p": [
     "Bulkeley and seven co-authors (including Frantzeskaki, who teaches this lecture) start from the rise of the 'experimental city': the idea that urban sustainability is increasingly governed through experiments, from new governance arrangements and demonstration projects to transition management and grassroots innovation (pp. 1–2). Experimentation appeals where the capacity to govern is fragmented and where what counts as improving the city is uncertain and contested. But as research grew, it became clear that experimentation is not one phenomenon that a single concept can capture. So the authors zoom in on one mode: the urban living laboratory (ULL). (Page numbers refer to the 1–19 pagination of the online-first version provided.)",
     "In descriptive terms ULLs share five features: they are embedded in a specific urban place, have an explicit learning function, involve participation and users, use alternative forms of leadership and ownership, and aim at evaluation and ongoing improvement (p. 3). They are promoted by EU 'lighthouse' programmes and national funders, used in partnerships between municipalities and universities, and used by community groups to secure legitimacy and resources. Yet the literature remains broad-brush, relying on a few emblematic examples. Two questions are open: why ULLs emerge and who is involved (and excluded), and how they actually work in practice and with what outcomes.",
     "The theoretical lens is pragmatist. Following John Dewey, experimentation is a form of 'inquiry': a situation is problematised as undetermined and then given a determination and a solution (p. 3). ULLs are therefore part of a politics of deciding which problems need solving and how. The authors develop a typology along two axes, how ULLs are designed and practised, and what 'disposition' towards the laboratory they take, and apply it to 40 European ULLs."

    "h": "What makes something a laboratory",
    "p": [
     "Laboratories have long been seen as uncontaminated spaces where science is done, but science studies shows they are historically contingent and take many forms (p. 4). The authors highlight two sets of relations. First, laboratories are spaces of 'improvement': an enhanced environment in which phenomena are manipulated towards desired characteristics, combining intervention with observation. That combination is what distinguishes a laboratory from other forms of novelty. Second, laboratories have a 'disposition' towards control and contingency. Borrowing from Easterling, a disposition is 'a propensity within a context' that lies not in the separate parts but in the relationships between them. A lab must be flexible enough to allow reconfiguration yet controlled enough to hold together (p. 4).",
     "ULLs can be read as governance in two ways (p. 5). From an institutional angle they are new governance arrangements. From a Foucauldian angle they are a form of 'governmentality': they manipulate socio-material conditions to shape conduct. Either way, their politics lie in how intervention and observation are designed and practised, and how the balance between control and contingency is set. The typology that follows is a heuristic of 'ideal types'; any real ULL will show features of several types and change over time (p. 5)."
    ]

    "h": "Three designs: strategic, civic and organic",
    "p": [
     "The design axis draws on Karvonen and van Heur's notions of situatedness and change orientation (pp. 5–6). Situatedness refers to who leads and how the urban is positioned relative to the lab: a lab must be grounded in a particular place, yet its results are meant to be valid anywhere (a 'placelessness'). On change orientation the authors disagree with Karvonen and van Heur, who see labs as intentionally radical. They \"suggest that such goals for urban improvement are not necessarily radical in nature and indeed can seek to reinforce the status quo\" (p. 6). Instead they study the 'politics of improvement': how priorities are set, which techniques of change dominate, and how it is financed.",
     "This yields three ideal types (Table 1, pp. 6–7). Strategic ULLs are steered by national or regional government and involve large corporate partners, modelled on national innovation programmes (Innovate UK, FFG, RVO, Vinnova, JPI Urban Europe). The city is a live 'test-bed' for other national and corporate priorities, often won through competitions and funded by a one-off lump sum. Civic ULLs centre on municipal priorities, civic universities and local firms: economic performance, jobs, fixing local infrastructure. They are typically co-funded by the municipality and partners, and to get national money must rework local priorities through national ones.",
     "Organic ULLs respond to specific needs of communities or neighbourhoods, such as unemployment, pollution or fuel poverty (p. 7). Civil society organisations lead: NGOs, charities, grassroots and community groups. This resonates with the grassroots innovation literature. Budgets are limited, depend on competitive bidding and are matched by volunteer time, so activities tend to be one-off projects or, at best, a programme sustained over time. (The paper uses 'organic' and 'grassroots' interchangeably for this type.)"

    "h": "Four dispositions: from control to contingency",
    "p": [
     "The second axis orders ULLs along a spectrum from tightly controlled to highly contingent, with four ideal-type dispositions (pp. 7–9). The trial is the most controlled: it tests products or processes under 'real world' conditions by limiting indeterminacy, confining the ULL in space and time. \"It is at heart a form of disciplinary power\" (p. 8), and it usually needs the market protection and political support associated with innovation niches. The enclave is also controlled but allows some contingency: it draws a spatial boundary around an experimental neighbourhood or district, treated as a microcosm of the whole yet set apart from it. The boundary becomes an 'obligatory passage point': any lesson must cross it before it can be replicated elsewhere.",
     "Where contingency outweighs control, the demonstration appears (p. 9). It is a showcase or exhibition of what the city could look like under the right conditions. Power works not through discipline but through 'government', shaping the 'conduct of conduct' (Foucault). It establishes potential without determining how it is used. The platform is the most contingent disposition. It does not fix relations but creates conditions under which many new relations can form, removing frames between the 'inside' and 'outside' of the experiment. Like digital platforms, it curates connectivity: previously disconnected actors are brought together on a shared stage, and boundaries are only loosely maintained (p. 9)."

    "h": "Evidence: 40 European living labs",
    "p": [
     "The empirical base comes from the GUST project (JPI Urban Europe). Each partner compiled ten two-page 'snapshot' cases for its country (Austria, the Netherlands, Sweden, the UK) from policy documents, websites and academic sources; some became in-depth studies with fieldwork, interviews, focus groups and workshops (pp. 9–10). Cases had to show the five ULL features, whether or not they used the label. The team assigned each case to the type and disposition it most resembled, deliberating over mixed cases. The authors acknowledge the sample comes from four countries 'quite advanced' in urban experimentation.",
     "The findings (Table 2, pp. 10–12): national patterns differ. Austria and Sweden show the full range; the UK has variety but no trials; in the Netherlands ULLs are mostly civic. Trials are rare, even though living labs originated in corporate testing of technologies. Design and disposition are linked: strategic ULLs are mostly demonstrations (E.ON's Hållbarheten apartment house in Malmö) or platforms (Future by Lund); organic ULLs are enclaves or platforms (Stapeln in Malmö); civic ULLs use all four. The platform appears across all three types, reflecting dispersed resources and cross-cutting challenges. Most common of all is the civic demonstration, which lets coalitions 'lead by example', move prototypes into real contexts and put their city 'on the map' (p. 12)."

    "p": [
     "Aspern Urban Lakeside, Vienna, is a strategic demonstration (pp. 12–13): one of Europe's largest development projects, a 'city within a city' on a 240-hectare former airfield, run by the city and the development agency Wien 3420 AG, with roughly €5 billion of public and private money over 2010–2028. The agency acts as 'bridge builder', attracting partners and marketing the site; smart grids, e-bikes and smart housing are showcased. The Aspern Citylab adds some circumscribed contingency through participatory design. Stapeln (STPLN), Malmö, is a community platform (pp. 13–14): a cultural house with maker workshops, started by the municipality and transferred to an NGO. Funding is patched together from the municipality (premises, 2.75 salaries), foundations and fees. The platform lets a resource-poor initiative link one-off activities into a programme.",
     "MK: Smart, Milton Keynes, is a civic platform (pp. 15–16). It began with a 2013 HEFCE call; lead shifted from the municipality to the Open University, a tactical move under austerity. A Data Hub curates real-time data across energy, transport and ICT projects, and the OurMK platform and a citizens' ideas competition funded 11 projects. Concept House Village, Rotterdam, is a civic demonstration (pp. 16–17): started in 2011 by universities in Heijplaat, a marginal area left open by the economic crisis, to test sustainable building and retrofit. As the economy recovered, the municipality wanted to move the site back into the mainstream: 'You got it for five years'. Lesson: ULLs often achieve different things than planned, and \"new thinking about their 'failure' will be needed\" (p. 17)."

    "h": "Conclusions and what the authors want",
    "p": [
     "The first conclusion is that ULLs lean towards contingent rather than controlled dispositions: demonstrations and platforms, not formal trials (p. 17). This reflects how governing capacity is configured. Municipalities, knowledge institutions and community groups work with limited autonomy and resources, while strategic political and economic actors lack legitimacy or capacity to enrol everything needed on the ground. It also reflects the open-ended nature of sustainability transformations. ULLs are thus a form of pragmatic 'inquiry' under indeterminacy, where varied and contested urban futures are in play.",
     "The second conclusion is more critical (p. 18). Most ULLs have an ambiguous relation with the idea of a transition pathway. Demonstrations offer a 'pre-figurative display' of a sustainable future in a building or district, with little attention to scaling up or rolling out. Platforms prioritise the capacity to run programmes of experiments rather than translating learning into wider transitions. \"The transformative potential of these dominant dispositions of ULLs seems to be much more modest than the claims made, and the links with radical visions of transition pathways somewhat limited\" (p. 18). Future research should study ULLs elsewhere, including outside Europe; how different types co-exist in one city; and whether ULLs can think beyond dominant visions and the interests of the most powerful actors."
