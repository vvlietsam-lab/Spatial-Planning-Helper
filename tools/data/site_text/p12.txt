    "p": [
     "Durrant (UCL), Lamker (Groningen) and Rydin (UCL) write a short 'Comment' in Planning Theory & Practice. They start from the revived debate on whether pursuing economic growth is compatible with meaningful cuts in greenhouse gas emissions and resource use, a debate labelled 'degrowth' or 'post-growth' (p. 287). Their question is practical: what do these debates mean for planning, and how could its existing instruments and tools be adapted, in quite radical ways, for a post-growth future? They use two core planning tasks as examples: housing and infrastructure.",
     "They deliberately narrow the scope (p. 287). They distinguish structural constraints on planning, the wider planning system, and the instruments and tools planners use, and focus on the last. Structural reform of societies and economies is left to a broader post-growth debate. They also set aside, 'for now', the relationship between lower- and higher-income countries, although rich countries may need to shrink their biophysical footprint by 40 to 50 percent. They argue their focus on what planners can do today is relevant in both the Global North and South."

    "h": "Planning's bond with growth",
    "p": [
     "Since 19th-century industrialisation, planning tools were developed to cope with, organise and even foster growth (pp. 287–288). In Europe and North America planning grew out of public health, housing conditions and infrastructure for growing cities; in colonial contexts it served economic growth through exploitation of colonised places and people. Recently economic growth has moved to the core of planning globally. The impetus for the paper is the recognition that \"planning has bound itself into a pro-growth agenda that is mentally and institutionally hard to overcome\" (p. 288). Instruments now facilitate development to achieve other goals, through direct benefits (infrastructure provision) or indirect ones (a bigger tax base), and success is usually expressed in money, as with GDP.",
     "The authors grant that GDP says something about livelihoods and wellbeing, and that the only experience of shrinking economies is recession, with unequal harm (p. 288). But they reject the idea that criticising growth means a forced recession: a planned restructuring focused on environmental and spatial change is different, and would reuse and adapt existing planning tools. The sharpest point concerns sustainability: \"It is particularly worrying to see how the pursuit of sustainability has been subsumed to the pro-growth agenda\" (p. 288). Planning embraced eco-modernism (eco-cities, carbon-neutral developments, renewable energy infrastructure), making it 'almost always possible' to label more development as sustainable. Trading off environmental, social and economic dimensions has avoided the deeper conflicts over resource use that Campbell identified."

    "h": "Post-growth, degrowth and the decoupling problem",
    "p": [
     "The authors distinguish the two labels (pp. 288–289). Degrowth is more rooted in economics, more structural and theoretical, and gives voice to North-South inequality and different development pathways. Post-growth is an interdisciplinary and pragmatic search for radical alternatives within existing structures, so it suits an analysis of today's planning tools while still aiming to transform planning itself. Both share one empirical core: the question of decoupling, meaning breaking the link between economic activity and resource use. Historically, growth has come with rising resource use and emissions despite efficiency gains, and reducing resource consumption matters as much as reducing emissions.",
     "Decoupling can be relative (the economy grows faster than resource use, thanks to efficiency) or absolute (resource use plateaus or falls while the economy grows) (p. 289). Most analysts say absolute decoupling is needed, but there is little evidence it is happening or even possible in a pro-growth world. The reasons: rebound effects (the Jevons paradox: efficiency does not reduce total consumption), technological change that misses high-consumption areas or shifts the problem elsewhere, an overestimated potential of recycling and the circular economy, and underestimated impacts of the service economy and of shifting consumption across the globe. Cities that market themselves as sustainability beacons may look worse once their residents' consumption emissions are counted (p. 289)."

    "h": "What post-growth planning would do for housing",
    "p": [
     "The central aim is stated plainly: \"The aim of post-growth planning is to delink planning policy and practice from the reliance on growth and ever-continuing new urban development to achieve public goals\" (p. 290). Planning should encourage resource efficiency, set a built environment that allows slower growth elsewhere in the economy, and fit a slower pace of development. At the same time, economic activity is a prerequisite for livelihoods, so planning should support sufficient activity and help redress the uneven spatial distribution of too little and too much, within absolute ecological limits. The toolbox considered: plan-making at several scales, regulation, taxation and incentives, information and assessments, public land ownership and direct development.",
     "One tool is explicitly excluded: land value capture, because it depends on creating more monetised development value, often in expensive locations, to fund other goals, which is inherently pro-growth (p. 290). Planning should also make literal space for low-value and non-commercial activities such as care and the social economy. For housing, post-growth planning rejects the idea, 'for which there is no foundation', that affordability can be solved by more market-led housebuilding in high-price areas. A first step is to diversify tenures and use site allocations to protect land for public, social and community housing, with ambitious targets for self-build and community-built housing, rather than tying social housing to a share of market housing."

    "h": "Efficiency, ceilings, shrinkage and the existing stock",
    "p": [
     "Two further pathways are needed (pp. 290–291). The first is regulating new development for the highest resource efficiency and circularity. Many systems already do this, but it needs better information: life cycle analysis and carbon accounting should be standard for every proposal, with attention to cumulative impacts at local, regional and national level so that individually 'good' projects do not add up to boundary-busting totals. More contentious: both minimum and maximum space standards. The second, more challenging pathway addresses the Jevons paradox: slowing and redistributing development. That means absolute limits, with ceilings and not only floors or targets, ideally framed nationally, and diverting activity from high-price, high-demand areas to lower-price, high-need areas.",
     "This implies abandoning the idea of growth engines, growth nodes in polycentric plans and competition between localities, which leads to over-supply (p. 291). Plans should consider the whole range from shrinkage through no or low growth to limited development. Shrinking areas could be testbeds for post-growth ideas, but shrinkage is not in itself success, and responsibility should not be pushed onto them alone; fast-growing areas may be better placed to lead. Planning should also make courageous use of the existing stock: requiring reuse where the carbon analysis supports it, using fiscal tools against excessive use of space, and restricting second homes and short-term lets."

    "h": "Infrastructure: planning for phase-out",
    "p": [
     "Infrastructure shapes the environmental impact of mobility, energy and water use, and building and maintaining it is resource-intensive (pp. 291–292). There can be strong post-growth arguments for replacing infrastructure to enable more efficient patterns, but also for working within existing networks rather than expanding them indefinitely, since they embody sunk resources and financial and political capital. Decisions will be delicate and context-specific. Better knowledge of embedded carbon and cumulative impacts is needed, along with critical scrutiny of the 'do nothing' scenarios that often justify new projects.",
     "Above all, new infrastructure should be coordinated with the planned replacement or shrinkage of existing infrastructure (p. 292). Investment in renewable energy should go with phasing out carbon-based infrastructure; expanding cycling and walking networks should take space from roads and car-based spaces; investment in long-distance rail should be coordinated with reduced air travel capacity. The authors admit this goes beyond tools and needs a new vision for planning. For now they seek 'hacks' that permit change from within. The main obstacle is that pro-growth thinking grips planners' imagination so strongly that alternatives seem inconceivable."

    "h": "Frameworks and the planner's role",
    "p": [
     "Post-growth planning needs supportive frameworks, especially nationally (p. 292). Planners have skills at community and neighbourhood level and can leverage local civil society; freed from pressure to accommodate market-led growth, these capacities could grow. But control over finance and land must be devolved within supportive regional, national and ideally international frameworks, for instance to meet housing needs through public, social or community schemes. On responsibility, the authors are careful (p. 293). Academics should show future practitioners that growth-based development is often incompatible with addressing the environmental crisis. Yet most planners are employees, often under elected politicians, so the authors question how far individual planners can legitimately promote a new agenda.",
     "Their answer is to ground the role in expertise rather than values (pp. 293–294). Planners know more than ever about resources and impacts, so instead of promoting market-led development with cursory impact assessment, they should spell out the medium- to long-term environmental and social consequences of plans, including the trade-offs and compensation usually offered. They should speak to the infeasibility of plans that presuppose growth, rather than follow standard 'best practices' of green growth. Just as it is unethical to propose an unimplementable plan, it is unethical to advise action known to be disastrous: \"Planners should consider part of their role to voice uncomfortable truths and to avoid the easier path or discourse of growth-dependence\" (p. 293). Planners might turn to supporting community initiatives, re-tooling towards community building and skills, and becoming holders of shared leadership (p. 294)."
