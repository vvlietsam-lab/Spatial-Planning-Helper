Part B defines the other institutionalisms (sociological, historical and evolutionary) and discusses institutional change. The learning objectives are to identify neo-institutionalist theories in planning, understand how they developed, and compare the philosophical and epistemological assumptions (assumptions about what exists and how we can know it) behind the different institutionalist paradigms.",
    "The starting definition is short: institutions are 'the formal and informal rules of the game'. The first point the lecture hammers home is that institutions are not organisations. Institutions are a system of established rules and norms which guide, constrain and regulate the behaviour of actors; organisations are concrete entities with a specific purpose. The Eurovision example makes this concrete: the European Broadcasting Union is the organisation, while its statutes and the formal and informal rules of the contest (the slide asks students to think of examples) are institutions."

   "h": "Why institutions matter to planners",
   "p": [
    "The slides quote Savini (2019): “Institutions are ontologically both the context and object of planning practices from a relational perspective”. Institutions as context means the framework within which planning decisions are made: laws, procedures, norms of what is appropriate.    "h": "Part A: from the tragedy of the commons to Ostrom",
   "p": [
    "The origins story starts with Hardin (1968). The 'tragedy of the commons' occurs when individuals acting in rational self-interest overuse and deplete a shared resource such as a pasture, a fish stock or the atmosphere. Each user gains from taking a bit more, while the cost of depletion is spread over everyone, so the rational individual choice produces a collectively bad outcome. The classic answers were privatisation or state control.",
    "Elinor Ostrom's work (Governing the Commons, 1990) asked a different question: how do self-interested people self-organise and create norms to prevent economically suboptimal outcomes? Her answer is institutions that overcome 'the problem of commitment': rules that make it credible that others will also hold back. She developed the Institutional Analysis and Development (IAD) framework, a structured way to analyse how rules shape an 'action situation' in which actors interact and produce outcomes.",
    "The IAD framework distinguishes seven types of rules. Position rules define the roles available; boundary rules specify how actors enter or leave a position (membership of a fishing cooperative); choice rules say what actions are allowed, obliged or forbidden (only certain gear); aggregation rules combine individual actions into collective decisions (majority voting in a village council); information rules regulate what must or may be shared (reporting catches); payoff rules assign rewards and sanctions (fines for breaking quotas); and scope rules define which outcomes are affected (off-limits areas, protected species). The slides link this to Bouwmeester and Hartmann (2021) on informal real estate markets in informal settlements."

   "h": "What neo-institutionalism is and where the strands disagree",
   "p": [
    "New or neo-institutionalism, according to the slides, offers three things: an explanatory theory of how institutions shape behaviour; an understanding of how institutions arise and transform; and a way of answering and reconciling the relationship between structure and agency, referring to Giddens. The planning example is Tennekes, Harbers and Buitelaar (2015), who show that institutional arrangements affect the morphology (physical form) of residential development in the Netherlands, Flanders and North Rhine-Westphalia.",
    "How do institutions influence behaviour? The slides stack several definitions. They constrain the set of possible actions and have a normative capacity to orient how agents interact (Giddens 1984); they define what is 'appropriate for actors to do within a given setting' (March and Olsen 2010); they 'guide and give meaning to human interaction' (Buitelaar 2014); and they are formal and informal norms and expectations about how to act in particular social contexts (Morgan and Engwall 1999). Following Sorensen (2017), the lecture then names three questions on which institutionalisms disagree: what institutions are, what they do, and how they transform, including their origins and whether they can be designed."

   "h": "Part B: rational choice, sociological and historical institutionalism",
   "p": [
    "Following Salet (2018), the lecture lists the three most important paradigms. Rational choice institutionalism uses North's (1990) definition: institutions are the formal and informal rules of the game, humanly devised constraints that shape human interaction. They coordinate effects and provide certainty, information and credible commitment, and they change when rational, self-interested actors create rules to enhance cooperation. Ostrom's commons work belongs here.",
    "Sociological institutionalism goes deeper into the mind. A slide quote (dated 1991) says “Institutions do not just constrain options: they establish the very criteria by which people discover their preferences.” Key ideas are 'goodness-of-fit', the reproduction of arrangements seen as legitimate because they fit established templates (DiMaggio and Powell 1983); institutions as symbol systems, cognitive scripts and moral templates that provide 'frames of meaning' (Hall and Taylor 1996); an internalised rather than external understanding; and the hegemonic character of norms and values. The example is Savini and Dembski (2016): the new development agenda for Amsterdam-Noord was enabled by a set of symbols linking the industrial shipyard past to a creative-economy future, showing planning as creating meaning and building consensus.",
    "Historical institutionalism, again via Hall and Taylor (1996), sees institutions as formal or informal procedures, routines, norms and conventions embedded in the organisational structure of the polity or political economy. Its key concepts are path dependency and critical junctures, with an emphasis on structural elements. The example is Holsen (2021), who asks why public participation in Norwegian land-use planning has remained relatively weak despite decades of legislation meant to strengthen it, and answers with a path-dependent explanation. The evolutionary strand is not given its own definition slide; it enters through Moroni in the exercise and in the take-home messages."

    "The discussion part starts from 'planning as institutional design'. For Dembski and Salet: symbolic markers cannot be used voluntaristically, new institutions emerge through new cultural practices that become institutionalised, institutionalisation is ongoing normalisation and habituation, and “The art of planning is to find niches of underinstitutionalised space.” (p. 622 on the slide).",
    "For Sorensen: critical junctures are short periods with a heightened probability that agents' choices affect the outcome; path dependence is usually not 'lock-in' but continuous contestation within a relatively constrained range of options; and critical juncture analysis shows how actors use conjunctural crises to push particular changes.
