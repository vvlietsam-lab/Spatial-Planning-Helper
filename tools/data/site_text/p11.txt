    "h": "What the comment argues and why",
    "p": [
     "This is a short opinion piece (a 'Comment' of about three pages in Nature Cities) by two planners from Tsinghua University. The printed text does not show page numbers, so none are given here. Its claim is simple and strong: AI is not just another planning tool but a disruption that forces planners to reconsider the profession's core values. Planning has relied on empirical judgement, public participation and incremental adaptation; AI's data-driven analytics, automated decisions and predictive models challenge all three. By reducing the planner's role in negotiating complex interests and obscuring who is responsible for outcomes, AI \"threatens to erode public trust and professional legitimacy\". Harnessing it responsibly, the authors argue, requires critical engagement with its ethical, democratic and equity implications."

    "h": "From CAD and GIS to 'urban AI'",
    "p": [
     "Planning has faced technological disruptions before: computer-aided design in the 1980s and GIS in the 1990s. These changed how planners visualise and analyse data but largely preserved the human role of interpretation and negotiation. Earlier 'smart city' paradigms mostly added incremental ICT solutions. 'Urban AI', by contrast, uses machine learning that can shape governance and physical planning more autonomously. Examples: Singapore optimises traffic signals with AI, Copenhagen forecasts energy use to cut emissions, and Los Angeles is exploring machine learning to allocate housing more fairly.",
     "The decisive difference: \"Rather than just supporting planners, AI stands apart by automating critical thinking tasks that were once exclusive to human professionals.\" The question is no longer whether AI can help with data, but whether it replaces deliberation, negotiation and contextual knowledge, the core of the planner's craft. Large language models sharpen this, because they can reason about broader context and value frameworks, not just domain-specific datasets."

    "h": "Four disruptive factors",
    "p": [
     "The authors name four disruptions. First, erosion of planners' judgement and expertise. Planners build judgement by interpreting community values, reconciling conflicts and adapting policies to context. Once validated, algorithms tend to become authorities: if an AI suggests an 'optimal' zoning mix, human intervention may be sidelined in the name of efficiency. \"In effect, the profession risks deskilling as reliance on algorithmic outputs displaces accumulated practitioner wisdom.\" Second, algorithmic bias. AI is sold as impartial, but real datasets carry social, economic and historical biases; deployed uncritically, AI can reinforce redlining, marginalise under-represented voices and ignore cultural or community values that numbers do not capture.",
     "Third, diminishing democratic engagement. Especially in the US, planning rests on public participation; automated, 'optimal' solutions can short-circuit slower deliberation, leaving stakeholders fewer opportunities or reasons to engage and cutting out voices that cannot be quantified. Fourth, blurred accountability. Planners balance technical feasibility, law and moral responsibility, but AI puts distance between a recommendation and its human overseers. If an automated site selection triggers gentrification, who is responsible? Without transparent design, open data and a clear chain of accountability, errors are hard to trace, producing a crisis of professional legitimacy."

    "h": "Why the disruption is unavoidable, and the counter-arguments",
    "p": [
     "Even if planners resist, market forces and public enthusiasm push AI forward. Developers use AI analytics to optimise returns, transport companies pilot AI routing, and politicians who want an 'innovative' city sponsor AI pilots from traffic signals to land-use compliance checks. Agencies that do not keep up risk becoming uncompetitive. Generative AI extends the reach beyond numbers to text analysis of policy documents, generative design and real-time sentiment analysis, so AI enters every level of practice.",
     "The authors engage two counter-arguments. One says AI augments rather than replaces planners, freeing them for strategy, facilitation and value judgements. The other says AI can be regulated through transparent algorithms and oversight committees. Both have merit but understate AI's disruptive force: in journalism and manufacturing, supportive tools evolved towards partial or full replacement, and the promise of speed and savings can override longer-term social goals. Agencies often lack the resources and skills for rigorous algorithmic audits, especially under political pressure to innovate. So balanced 'augmentation' is precarious."

    "h": "Lessons for practice and the foundations of planning",
    "p": [
     "If disruption is unavoidable, the profession should set the terms. The authors propose five lessons. Reclaim interpretive agency: planners as 'sense-makers' who interpret AI outputs in light of local histories, cultures and values, with ethical frameworks similar to doctors using clinical decision-support. Embed community values: involve diverse stakeholders from the start, with marginalised groups in data collection, model building and validation ('community-in-the-loop'). Strengthen regulatory oversight: standards for algorithmic transparency and bodies that audit and certify AI tools, as in finance and healthcare. Redefine education: digital literacy, AI basics, data science and ethics alongside policy, design and engagement skills. Resist over-reliance: 'slow planning' based on face-to-face deliberation as a counterweight to computational speed.",
     "Finally, the authors return to planning's foundations: balancing individual and collective interests, shaping space according to democratic values and anticipating the future ethically. \"AI disrupts these foundations by recasting city-making as a purely technical optimization problem.\" They grant that AI might help balance interests, provided humans keep oversight. AI is thus the profession's 'starkest crossroads' in decades: a chance to reinvent planning's relevance, or a crisis that reduces humans to an afterthought. The aim is not rejecting AI but deliberately rethinking its integration, so that it remains a tool serving human aspirations, which requires alliances among communities, academics, policymakers and software developers."
