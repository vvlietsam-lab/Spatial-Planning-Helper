    "h": "What the article asks and why",
    "p": [
     "This is the introduction to a 2020 special issue of Urban Studies, and its title question is meant seriously: why does everyone now believe that cities can save the planet? Angelo and Wachsmuth start from a puzzle. Today the 'sustainable city' leads urban planning and policy; as Parnell puts it, 'there is no longer a question of whether cities are important for sustainable development, but rather why and how the urban condition affects our common future' (p. 2202). Yet in 1999 the environmentalist Herbert Girardet could still describe the relationship between cities and sustainability as a potential contradiction in terms (p. 2202). What happened in between?",
     "The authors do not set out to prove or disprove that cities are sustainable. Their aim is historical and critical: to explain how a shift in the environmental imaginary of the city took place, 'from the city as a sustainability problem to the city as a sustainability solution' (p. 2203), and to prevent that shift from being 'naturalised', that is, treated as obvious and inevitable. They build on their earlier critique of 'methodological cityism' (urban-environmental research that looks only inside city boundaries) and now add a time dimension."

    "h": "From problem to solution: the new common sense",
    "p": [
     "The introduction sketches how the consensus formed. After the Brundtland report (1987) and the Earth Summit, 'sustainable city' initiatives spread in the 1990s. Girardet pioneered an urban view of sustainable development; Jenks and colleagues argued for the 'compact city', based on the insight that variations in urban form are systematically linked to socio-environmental outcomes (p. 2203; the lecture 2 slides quote this line); the US 'smart growth' movement applied this to suburban contexts. The idea that density is green was still counterintuitive in 2004 when David Owen popularised it, but it has since become the basis of municipal and national climate plans worldwide (p. 2203).",
     "The authors note that this happened even while suburban growth continued to expand across the globe. Smart city thinking carries the same assumption (the built environment can be re-engineered to improve local and global sustainability) with added techno-optimism, and 'resiliency' reframed it again around crisis. The result is that sustainability understood as dense, green urbanism has become the norm (p. 2203). Critics have examined its governance, economics, ideology and inequalities; this article asks where the vision came from."

    "h": "Root 1: sprawl in the Global North",
    "p": [
     "The core argument is that three historical developments, rooted in the 1970s and intensifying in the 1990s, together made cities look like the solution: sprawl, informal settlements and climate change (p. 2204). Each followed the same pattern: the city and nature were first seen as opposed, and later as potentially in harmony. Although sprawl and informal settlements are now understood as global problems, strategies to address them long developed separately, in different professional and geographical spheres (p. 2204).",
     "Sprawl is the first. The US, the most car-dependent country, is the key site (pp. 2204–2205). For a long time suburbs were themselves the proposed cure for the ills of dense, polluted tenements. From the 1960s, inspired by Silent Spring, The Closing Circle and The Limits to Growth, environmentalists saw suburban sprawl as unsustainable resource use. Importantly, these critics treated suburbanisation as equal to urbanisation in general and blamed economic growth: their critiques were often anti-capitalist rather than just anti-suburban. Callenbach's novel Ecotopia (1975), where cars and 'economic progress' are abolished, is the example (p. 2205).",
     "The shift to the city as solution began with the 1970s oil crisis and grew in the 1980s and 1990s as cities tried to lure the white middle class back downtown and as concern about emissions grew. Architect Peter Calthorpe wrote in 1985: 'Ideally, the city is the most environmentally benign form of human settlement' (p. 2205). Newman and Kenworthy, Jenks et al. and the Sierra Club's 1998 'Challenge to Sprawl' followed. What unites them is that they separate sprawl (the problem) from dense urban development (the solution) (p. 2206)."

    "h": "Root 2: informal settlements in the Global South",
    "p": [
     "The second development played out in a different professional world: international development. By the 1970s informal settlements were growing rapidly across the Global South without the industrialisation that was supposed to replace them, and they came to be seen as environmental problems: pollution, exposure to floods and landslides, disease (p. 2206). Global policy documents show this framing, from the 1976 Vancouver Declaration (Habitat I) and Brundtland's chapter on 'Third World Cities' to Habitat II (1996) and UN-Habitat's The Challenge of Slums (2003). The response was to clear or 'upgrade' settlements in the name of development (p. 2207).",
     "Then the narrative turned. UN-Habitat's 2010 report called urbanisation a positive force for transformation, and Stewart Brand's article 'How slums can save the planet' described informal settlements as 'unexpectedly green' because of their extreme density, walkability, recycling and community networks (p. 2207). Earlier work by Gilbert et al. (1996) had even argued that the North might need to become more like the South. Dharavi became a symbol of creativity, and Aravena won the Pritzker Prize in 2016 for housing based on informal incremental design. The very features once seen as problems (informality, impermanence, living with less, density) were now taken up as models for sustainable living (p. 2207)."

    "h": "Root 3: climate change connects North and South",
    "p": [
     "Climate change is the third development and the one that ties the other two together, providing, in Slavin's words, the context for the global institutionalisation of urban sustainability (p. 2208). Brundtland had credited Northern cities, in theory, with the flexibility and local leadership to act, but spent more words on their disproportionate resource use and pollution (p. 2208). Through the 1980s and 1990s cities were first seen as climate problems: key sources of emissions because of their growing share of population and the consumption of affluent residents.",
     "International treaties struggled. The UNFCCC (1992) was non-binding; Kyoto's (1997) targets were political compromises with little scientific basis; the US withdrew under Bush and later from Paris under Trump (p. 2208). As national efforts floundered, affluent Northern cities were cast as a great hope because of nimble leadership, direct accountability and a feasible scale of action (p. 2208). The 1992 Rio conference recognised cities as an arena for sustainability. City networks followed: ICLEI (1990), the US Mayors' Climate Protection Agreement (2007), C40, 100 Resilient Cities, the Global Covenant of Mayors (p. 2209).",
     "A second climate wave focused on vulnerability. Hurricanes Katrina, Sandy, Harvey and Irma, droughts in São Paulo and Cape Town and floods in South Asia led to 'resiliency' planning and investment, such as New York's Rebuild By Design and the export of Dutch water expertise, and by private foundations such as the Rockefeller Foundation's 100 Resilient Cities. Today, city leaders see their cities as both unusually vulnerable to climate change and significantly responsible for it, so the profile of climate issues has become distinctly urban (p. 2209)."

     "The authors then summarise the three stories in their Table 1 (p. 2210). Anti-urban, back-to-the-land visions exist but are 'paths not taken'.     "p": [
     "The second half proposes how to study 'cities saving the planet' critically, framing the special issue (p. 2212). Historical research places urban-environmental concerns in their time and path dependencies.      "Multi-spatial research looks beyond city limits: the relation between city, suburb, periphery and countryside, such as Freiburg's and Calgary's central 'sustainability fix' met with a suburban 'counter-sustainability fix', the reconfiguration of the commons at Delhi's urban-rural edge, the colonial and postcolonial pathways along which climate adaptation expertise travelled from Amsterdam to New York and Jakarta, and the invisible 'operational landscapes' of energy, extraction, logistics and waste that support cities (pp. 2213–2214).",
     "Political research asks who gains. The authors note a shift from the anti-capitalist 'limits to growth' tradition to 'sustainability as the sustainability of growth' (p. 2215). Representational research studies the images and ideologies (green versus grey urban nature, techno-utopian bird's-eye views, model cities such as Bogotá, 'mayors ruling the world') that make policies look like common sense (pp. 2215–2216)."
