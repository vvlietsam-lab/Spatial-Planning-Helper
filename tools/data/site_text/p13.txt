    "h": "What the article asks and why",
    "p": [
     "Green and blue spaces are increasingly treated as solutions to climate adaptation, biodiversity loss, public health and social cohesion (p. 2402). EU policy frames them as 'green infrastructure', 'ecosystem services' and 'nature-based solutions': the Green Deal, the Biodiversity Strategy 2030 calling for greening in every European city, the Green City Accord, and the New Leipzig Charter, which also asks for citizen participation and co-creation. Citizens press politicians to act, and research has turned to justice issues such as the effects of greening on vulnerable groups. With policy, research and society all pushing, green space planning has 'tailwind'. But is there evidence that it has actually improved in European cities over the past decade?",
     "Hansen and 26 co-authors answer with a longitudinal study (p. 2403). In 2014 the EU project GREEN SURGE studied green space planning in 20 European cities. This follow-up returns to eleven of them and asks two questions: how have urban green space planning and governance changed, and what influenced these changes? And what is the contribution of current green space planning and governance to urban transformations? The title states the tension: are the changes transformative, or piecemeal?"

    "h": "Planning, governance and what counts as transformation",
    "p": [
     "The authors first define terms (p. 2403). Green space planning means formal, government-led processes to protect, enhance and create public green and blue space using instruments such as strategic plans, regulations and action programmes. Green space governance stresses the involvement of multiple actors, often decentralised, networked and participatory. Following Arnouts et al., there is a continuum from hierarchical government-led planning, via co-governance, to self-governance. The analysis of change uses three clusters inherited from GREEN SURGE: contextual factors (planning system, politics, resources), planning approaches (themes and instruments), and governance and participation, plus a question on COVID-19 (pp. 2403–2404).",
     "To judge whether changes are transformative, the paper draws on transformation theory (p. 2404). Transformative change is \"intentional, actively pursued, radical and systemic\" and requires breaking through path dependencies and changing or replacing institutions, structures and practices. Municipal politicians and officials can steer such change, but planning is also constrained by established paradigms, multi-level legal frameworks, siloed institutions, routines and past investments, and municipalities often lack resources and knowledge. The authors note that many transformation frameworks do not fit easily with planning's traditional rationale of stability and rational land-use guidance (pp. 2404–2405). (They use 'transformation' for both the transitions and transformations literatures.)",
     "Their yardstick is the four targeted outcomes of urban transformation from Wolfram, Borgström and Farrelly (p. 2405). Innovation: creating, nurturing and anchoring novelties. Exnovation: exposing and dismantling path dependencies ('unlocking'). Co-governance (originally 'collective stewardship'): enabling and aligning diverse actors across sectors and scales. Social justice: ensuring diversity and contestation, since technocratic decisions often unintentionally reinforce inequities, which calls for redistributing resources and decision-making power (pp. 2405–2406). A deliberate transformation should aim at least one of these."

    "h": "Method: re-interviewing eleven cities",
    "p": [
     "The study used structured expert interviews with open and closed questions, mirroring the 2014 approach (p. 2406). There was no funding, so researchers took part voluntarily and case selection was pragmatic: volunteers were found for eleven of the 20 cities. Interviewers knew the local language and context. In seven cities the same person was interviewed as in 2014, typically a municipal official leading green space planning; in four a person in a similar role. Interviews ran from August 2020 to March 2021, lasted 60 to 130 minutes, and were done face-to-face (two), by phone (three) or online (six). Results represent the interviewee's view.",
     "Analysis had three phases (pp. 2406–2407): an overview of the extent of change using mainly closed answers; an inductive, in-depth coding of open answers compared with the 2014 'case study portraits', condensed into four trends (global trends and disruptive events, urban growth, integrated planning, governance); and a discussion against the four transformation outcomes. The cities, which include Aarhus, Barcelona, Berlin, Bristol, Edinburgh, Helsinki, Linz, Lisbon, Ljubljana, Malmö and Utrecht, cover five European planning clusters and range from about 200,000 inhabitants (Linz) to over 3.6 million (Berlin) (pp. 2407–2408). They are not a random sample: many are EU research partners and sustainability champions, and so good cases to look for orchestrated transformation."

    "h": "Results: more attention, more plans",
    "p": [
     "Contextual change was mostly seen as positive (pp. 2408–2409). National political change was positive in six of eleven cities (new environmental laws, ecological issues in land-use planning), as was local change in six, sometimes due to new political leadership. Resources were mixed: positive in three cities, ambivalent in three, negative in two, with budget cuts and maintenance budgets not keeping pace with new green space. Five cities reported greater awareness of climate change or of green space's value for health, well-being or justice; three reported tension between greening and densification.",
     "In planning approaches (pp. 2409–2411), the most important instruments were unchanged in eight of eleven cities, often statutory master plans or green space plans. Almost all themes grew in relevance: climate change in ten cities, biodiversity and human health in eight each. Climate adaptation sparked the most new policies, partly after floods and heat waves, and biodiversity concern led to new plans. For other themes, no new instruments were reported. Awareness of social cohesion rose in seven cities, but only two reported more awareness of cultural diversity. Ecosystem services and green infrastructure were more integrated in seven cities each; action plans such as tree planting programmes improved in six. In governance (p. 2411), municipal participation efforts increased in nine cities, interest from non-governmental actors in eight, and cross-departmental cooperation in nine."

    "h": "Trends and tensions",
    "p": [
     "Four deeper trends emerged. First, responses to global developments and disruptions (pp. 2411–2413): Barcelona's Green Infrastructure and Biodiversity Plan, Bristol translating the SDGs into its One City Plan, flooding in Aarhus, many non-statutory strategies (climate adaptation, stormwater, green roofs, a bee strategy in Berlin), climate or ecological emergency declarations, and regulation such as Helsinki's Green Area Factor. COVID-19 raised appreciation of green space, but only Barcelona reported concrete acceleration. Awareness of social cohesion grew, but specific green space actions for disadvantaged areas were few. Second, urban growth was ambiguous (p. 2413): densification brought resources for green space but also conflicts with affordable housing, sharpened by tourism in Berlin and Barcelona. Aarhus used 'exchange agreements' in which developers fund green space.",
     "Third, steps towards integrated planning (pp. 2413–2415). Climate adaptation and, in Berlin, sustainable mobility created opportunities; Utrecht described a shift from parks and peri-urban landscapes to connectivity and neighbourhood access, especially in lower-income areas, and created 'guilds' (gildes) linking urban development and green space planning. Helsinki and Linz merged units; Linz involved experts and stakeholders early. But Berlin separated urban and green space planning, even physically, and Malmö's integration increased workloads. Fourth, participation 'between tokenism and co-governance' (pp. 2415–2416): Ljubljana's NGOs mainly opposed projects; Berlin, Helsinki and Lisbon mostly involved citizens in government-led planning; Bristol struggled to meet demands for participation. Aarhus, Bristol, Edinburgh and Utrecht stood out, with Aarhus creating a sub-department for citizenship and cultural change, Utrecht's slogan 'Together we make Utrecht', and Friends of Parks groups in the UK."

    "h": "Discussion: four lenses on transformation",
    "p": [
     "The overall verdict is sober (p. 2416). Many changes were incremental, others ambiguous, so \"these championing cases of sustainable urban development in Europe cannot be classified as radical urban transformations\". Integration brought influence and resources but also trade-offs; higher budgets did not solve long-term maintenance; even COVID-19 produced only short-term change in one city. That cities struggled to use windows of opportunity shows the power of structural conditions and path dependencies in formal administrations. Through the four lenses: innovation took the form of new strategies and systems thinking, anchored in policy, though new strategies may also reveal that old instruments are inflexible and stuck on recreation and access (pp. 2416–2417).",
     "Exnovation was rare (p. 2417). The Aarhus unit for participation and cultural change was the clearest case, a form of change management; more cooperation between green space, planning, water and transport units may be a path towards dismantling silos. Co-governance increased, but participation mostly stayed at the lower rungs of information and consultation, partly due to lacking capacity and flexibility; where co-creation was embedded in high-level policy, perspectives of citizens and officials changed (pp. 2417–2418). Social justice was the weakest lens: awareness grew, but specific approaches were rare, and even in the one city whose new policies centre on social cohesion and equality, these goals had not yet reached green space planning. \"Compared to the wide range of planning responses dealing with ecological issues this lack of consideration of social issues in spatial planning is a considerable disparity\" (p. 2418)."

    "h": "Conclusions and what the authors want",
    "p": [
     "The conclusion balances progress and limits (pp. 2418–2419). Green space gets more attention, several cities are overcoming path dependencies such as policy silos, and some co-create green infrastructure with businesses and civil society. Deliberate transformative change happened at high levels of local policy-making, through new policies and organisational cultures for co-governance, and incremental changes (citizen interest, systems thinking, integrated planning) may seed future transformations. But long-term maintenance, funding and organisational culture remain challenges, and cultural diversity and other justice aspects are poorly recognised.",
     "Fundamentally, the authors write, the problem is limited resources and staff capacity caused by system-level conditions beyond the control of green space departments or even municipalities (p. 2419). They call for multi-scale policy frameworks supporting nature-based innovation and new partnerships, change management and training on social aspects, incentives from political or administrative leaders to identify and 'exnovate' lock-in practices, and putting social and environmental justice at the heart of green space planning to avoid technocratic, unjust outcomes. They admit a key limitation: the study relies on officials who may be either preservers of the status quo or agents of change. Future research should include other stakeholders, especially active citizen groups."
