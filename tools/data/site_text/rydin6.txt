Unlike governance theory (ch. 5), it does not assume conflicts can be handled to reach effective or agreed outcomes; it looks at power in processes and inequality in outcomes. Planners 'cannot be neutral' and should act more like advocates; the research task is how to plan justly. The perspective studies both elite and non-elite actors, and rejects pluralism (Dahl), in which group activism simply follows demand: pluralism came to be seen as 'naïve' because power and resources are unequal, some issues never get on the agenda, and it legitimated a focus on middle-class pressure groups.    "p": [
    "This chapter from Rydin's textbook Theory in Planning Research introduces the urban politics perspective as a framework for doing planning research. It starts from the insight that conflict is unavoidable in planning, quoting Trapenberg Frick: 'Turbulent debates between politically divergent actors are part of the fiber of planning' (p. 105). The contrast is with the governance framework of the previous chapter, which assumes conflicts can be handled to reach effective and even agreed outcomes. Urban politics instead sees power at work in planning processes and inequality in their outcomes. Its focus is equity, equality and power in how planning handles conflict (p. 105).",
    "This makes the perspective challenging and potentially normative. 'Planners cannot be neutral in this conceptual context' (p. 105): if planning may disadvantage the dispossessed, planners must act more like advocates than neutral technocrats, and justice becomes central. The perspective studies both elites and non-elites, because the position of those who gain little can only be understood by looking at those who gain most (pp. 105–106). The chapter follows a standard structure: framing the research, key concepts (growth coalitions and regimes, urban social movements, agonism), then worked examples of research papers, and conclusions on method."

   "h": "Beyond pluralism: activism, justice and the right to the city",
   "p": [
    "Rydin first distinguishes urban politics from pluralism, the view that groups form whenever there is demand to address an issue, so activism follows demand (p. 106). By the late twentieth century pluralism was seen as naïve: groups differed hugely in power and resources, some issues never became the focus of activism, and pluralism in effect legitimated a focus on middle-class pressure groups. Urban politics instead looks at activism outside institutionalised participation (petitions, meetings, lobbying), including non-violent occupations and even violence against property, which it treats as legitimate forms of protest by the disempowered (p. 106).",
    "A strong concern with justice runs through this work, especially environmental justice, which grew from US cases where toxic sites were placed near communities of colour, such as Love Canal (p. 106). Rydin summarises Agyeman's four aspects of justice (pp. 106–107): recognition of all groups as self-determined groups, not just giving them voice; fair processes, especially open-ended visioning; just exercise of procedures such as regulation and implementation; and fair outcomes. A second justice theme is the Right to the City movement, protesting limited access to the city and the inequitable use of its assets, for example the Occupy movement after 2008 (p. 107). Because these conflicts are linked to wider power and inequality, the perspective is more modest about what planning alone can achieve: justice may require wider political change (p. 107)."

   "h": "Key concepts 1: growth coalitions and urban regimes",
   "p": [
    "The research question for planning is whether planning processes support capitalist interests (facilitating development) or middle-class interests (protecting areas from it), and whether they fail other groups and social movements (p. 107). The task is not sociological but about how planners and planning systems respond to conflicts. The first concept is the growth coalition, proposed by Molotch in the 1970s: a coalition of those with most to gain from land and development decisions comes together to secure the political conditions for property-led growth, often with close links to local government (p. 108). It was a reaction against technocratic views of planning. Critics from political economy found it too agent-based; others found it too simple, since coalitions are fragmented, unstable, not only local, and not always growth-oriented (p. 108).",
    "From these critiques came urban regime theory, introduced by Stone in his study of Atlanta (p. 108). A regime emerges because local government cannot implement its agenda alone; actors pool resources because they are interdependent. The power involved is 'power to' achieve goals, not 'power over' others, and it works alongside formal politics, often invisibly. Stone defined a regime as 'an informal yet relatively stable group with access to institutional resources that enable it to have a sustained role in making governing decisions' (p. 108). Dowding et al. list eight empirical criteria, including a distinctive, long-lived agenda, a boundary-crossing coalition, survival of personnel changes, strong leadership and public-private partnerships (p. 109). Stone identified four regime types: maintenance or caretaker, development, middle-class progressive, and lower-class opportunity expansion (p. 109).",
    "Regime theory still has a corporate bias: to access the systemic power of business, political leaders must cater to corporate interests (Pierre, p. 109). This was typical of US cities, which rely on local property taxes and have much autonomy from central government, so transferability is questioned. Holman identifies four obstacles for comparative use: more diverse actors, national differences in local autonomy, different incentives for business to engage in city politics, and globalisation (p. 109). Much research therefore asks how the concept must be amended to travel."

   "h": "Key concepts 2: urban social movements",
   "p": [
    "The second concept came from left-leaning theorists disheartened by political economy's message that structural change requires crisis and revolution (p. 109). They sought a more incremental path by treating politics as having some autonomy from capitalist structures. Gramsci was the key inspiration: the working class and the disempowered can perceive their own interests and develop programmes without a vanguard (p. 110). The word 'movement' signals more than a pressure group: it represents a fundamental social cleavage.",
    "Castells theorised urban social movements (USMs) around 'the means of collective consumption': the public services needed to reproduce the labour force, such as housing and transport, which households cannot provide alone (p. 110). Movements resist both middle-class appropriation of services and their reduction to capitalist needs. Later work, for example Nicholls, linked USMs to cities as spaces of difference and freedom, and hence to identity politics and control over local spaces (pp. 110–111). Mayer describes four phases: industrial crisis (1960–1980), welfare state erosion and collective consumption (1980–1990), gentrification and regeneration (1990–2000), and globalisation and financialisation since 2000 (p. 111). Critiques note neglect of gender and a bias towards North America and Northern Europe. What remains distinctive is a physical base in a specific place (p. 111)."

   "h": "Key concepts 3: agonism and the persistence of conflict",
   "p": [
    "The third concept starts from a practical planning problem: civil society is diverse, so who represents 'the community' in governance networks (p. 111)? Planners tend to include self-organised groups, labelling others 'hard to reach' or 'silent', as if they were recalcitrant. Rydin asks what counts as a group at all: economic classes, self-identities (ethnicity, sexuality, status, politics), identities imposed and then reclaimed, and place-based interests. The term 'place attachment' is used partly to resist the pejorative label NIMBY (pp. 111–112).",
    "Drawing on Mouffe, agonism rejects governance claims that deliberation can move towards consensus as misleading (p. 112). Conflict is seen as inevitable and even productive; the us-and-them division remains, but the aim is for groups to co-exist with respect. 'In essence, agonism involves a willingness to agree to disagree' (p. 112): opponents become adversaries who recognise each other's point of view, which Mouffe sees as more democratic than the false promises of deliberation. It has attracted attention as a possible new normative theory after collaborative planning, and links to critiques of post-political planning (Raco). Critics ask whether agonism simply accepts existing power inequalities (Yamamoto), which has spurred research on how conflicts are handled in practice (p. 112)."
