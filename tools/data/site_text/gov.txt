   "h": "What the chapter asks and why",
   "p": [
    "Rydin's book is about the theories that sit, often unspoken, underneath planning research. Chapter 2 opens the series with the oldest and most familiar frame: the 'governmental' or 'public administration' model, in which planning is a rational activity of the state that links means to ends in the public interest. The chapter has three jobs. It explains why planning exists at all according to this frame, it sets out the key concepts the frame works with (evidence, comprehensive synthesis, implementation), and it shows through seven published studies what research looks like when it adopts this frame, often without saying so.",
    "The title already hints at Rydin's stance: this is 'the hope' of rational public administration. She does not dismiss the model; she treats it as an ideal that policy makers aspire to and that much research quietly shares. The question for you as a reader is: how do you recognise this model in a piece of research, and what does it take for granted? The 22 Sept lecture (Bossuyt) used the chapter exactly this way: as a tool for spotting the rational model in theses and articles."

   "h": "Why plan? Market failure and the public interest",
   "p": [
    "Rydin starts with the justification (p. 19). Planning histories present planning as a response to problems: slum housing, poor sewage, pollution in fast-growing cities. One influential account of those problems comes from welfare economics, whose key idea is 'market failure': the reasons why markets fail to deliver the broader public interest. She lists the sources. Externalities are impacts, positive or negative, that do not show up in prices; pollution is the classic negative one. Missing markets mean there is no price to ration use, so things like beautiful views get over-exploited; information is also under-supplied because you cannot easily charge for it.",
    "Welfare economics also identifies goods that markets under-supply: merit goods (things society decides people should have, such as good housing) and public goods (once supplied to some, others cannot be excluded, such as street lighting). Finally there is monopoly power, and in land-use planning the key monopoly is that of landowners, which can justify the public sector stepping in (pp. 19–20). The conclusion: planning redresses problems that market processes alone cannot solve (p. 20). The lecture summarised this chain as: markets allocate efficiently, except when they don't, so the state may intervene; the public interest then equals the greatest net benefit to society, and planning becomes the optimisation of welfare."

   "h": "The Weberian division: politicians set ends, planners choose means",
   "p": [
    "This justification puts the planner and other public officials centre stage, in the Weberian tradition (p. 20). Max Weber described the modern state as run by trained officials who follow impersonal rules and serve the political leadership. In Rydin's words: 'The goals themselves stand outside the planning process' (p. 20). Politicians, answerable to voters, set the goals; the public administration interprets them, considers how to implement them and brings expert knowledge to bear on the options and policy instruments. This is 'instrumental rationality', as the lecture put it with Alexander's definition: reason as linking means to ends, and being able to justify a course of action.",
    "Planning in this frame is purposeful problem-solving: urban planning improves towns, environmental planning protects nature, transport planning creates efficient mobility. Rydin notes that the smart city can be seen as the latest version, integrating technological rationality into urban planning (p. 20). Crucially, the frame assumes that good planning produces the desired outcomes, so any problems are located within the planning process and in how planners operate (p. 20). The frame looks inward at the process, not outward at the context in which planning works (p. 21)."

   "h": "The ideal model: Lichfield's eleven steps",
   "p": [
    "The model is also an idealised sequence of steps. Rydin uses Nathaniel Lichfield's 'general planning process' (Fig. 2.1, p. 21): (1) recognising and defining the problem, (2) deciding to act and defining the task, (3) data collection, analysis and forecasting, (4) constraints and objectives, (5) operational criteria for design, (6) plan design, (7) testing alternative plans, (8) plan evaluation, (9) decision-taking, (10) implementation, (11) review over time. Monitoring closes the loop, because outcomes feed back into how instruments are used. The lecture linked this to the CATS ten steps from the bachelor's: data, forecast, goals, alternatives, testing, choice, monitoring.",
    "Rydin condenses the ideal type in a short definition (p. 21): planning is future-oriented and comprehensive, emphasises synthesis and integration, is based on information, knowledge and evidence, responds to a public interest set in the political sphere, and involves stakeholders, but only instrumentally, to make planning more effective. That last clause matters for later readings: participation here is a means to better plans, not a source of legitimacy. The model is found in public documentation and professional guidance; it is an ideal that planning policy makers aspire to."

   "h": "Key concept 1: evidence-based planning",
   "p": [
    "Rydin identifies three dynamics that matter in this frame: evidence, comprehensive synthesis and implementation (p. 22). First, evidence. Planning should rest on the best current knowledge: data on the present situation, likely future scenarios and trends, and knowledge of how a specific development will have impacts. Her example is wind farms: knowledge about bird strikes, noise and flicker for residents, and for offshore sites, impacts on fish, fishing fleets and marine mammals. The core assumption is stated plainly: 'the assumption is that knowledge can be gathered and made available to the planning process in a positive way, supporting decision-making and the development of strategies' (p. 22). 'Positive' here means in the positivist sense: knowledge as neutral facts that can be collected.",
    "Big data and IT extend this hope: Kontokosta argues planners need new data and computational methods to forecast needs and evaluate alternatives (p. 22). Rational planning also needs knowledge of how policy itself works, which is the idea behind 'evidence-based planning': making policies work better by understanding how policy mechanisms bring about change (Sanderson 2002, quoted p. 22). Rydin flags a problem: many confounding factors, from markets to social dynamics, shape urban change, so the effectiveness of planning tends to be partly taken for granted (p. 23). She also cites Davoudi (2006) on the suggestion that policy often leads to the choice of evidence rather than the reverse."
   ]

  {
   "h": "Key concept 2: comprehensive synthesis",
   "p": [
    "Second, planning claims to take account of many dimensions at once (p. 23). A transport plan considers congestion, safety, environment and economic development; a regeneration plan aims at community, accessibility and mixed uses. The 'triple bottom line' of social, environmental and economic goals is often used to check comprehensiveness. But it is more than listing dimensions: planning must integrate them, for example designing a landscape that works as sustainable drainage while also being attractive, safe, accessible and not wasting development land (pp. 23–24). Planning is thus a synthesising activity that looks across all issues to produce a reasoned, balanced overall view (p. 24). Systems thinking (McLoughlin 1969) and later socio-ecological systems were proposed as ways to achieve this.",
    "Here Rydin states where legitimacy comes from in this model: stakeholder and public input may matter, 'but the legitimacy of plans and planning decisions comes from the rationality of the analysis and propositions that are put forward by planners and planning agencies' (p. 24). Tools such as environmental assessment, SEA, cost-benefit analysis, multi-criteria analysis and GIS fit this model; their proponents stress they inform rather than make decisions. The lecture's Dutch example was the MKBA (social cost-benefit analysis) of making the Westerscheldetunnel toll-free: a net welfare gain of 27–39 million euros, but some effects, such as the feeling of injustice about the toll, could not be put in euros. Rydin notes the state struggles with both too little and too much knowledge, which led to Lindblom's disjointed incrementalism (p. 24)."
   ]
  },
  {
   "h": "Key concept 3: from formulation to implementation",
   "p": [
    "Third, plans are not ends in themselves; they must be implemented (pp. 24–25). A whole evaluation industry judges planning bodies by whether they achieve stated goals, assuming a linear process: deliberate, predict, plan, formulate, implement, evaluate. Smith (2018) distinguishes 'plan implementation' (did the plan's goals happen?) from 'planning implementation' (closer to integration, needing process evaluation). Rydin lists the policy instruments that together measure the power of the state planner (p. 25): direct public investment including landownership, strategic coordination through spatial planning, regulation and permits, fiscal measures, tradeable permits, and information and persuasion including nudges.",
    "Implementation studies then complicate the picture from within. Outcomes depend not just on which tools are used but on how: fit with context, compatibility, structure and agency, resources and power, catalysts and communication (p. 25). Planners have discretion and negotiate with developers, communities and other departments. This blurs the Weberian line: goals get reformulated during implementation, so planners, as administrators, are inevitably political (p. 26). Kaza (2019) adds that not all plans should be judged by implementation at all, since plans can also frame debates, spread information or offer a narrative; some authorities even adopt mutually inconsistent plans (p. 26)."

   "h": "Conclusions and what the author wants",
   "p": [
    "Rydin's conclusion (pp. 37–38) is that the governmental approach frames planning as a linear, rational process with feedback loops, and this framing describes both how planning operates and how it should. Research in this frame stays within the model and typically becomes policy evaluation: goals are taken as given, the focus is on steps and instruments, and before-and-after data judge impact. In her phrase, research 'becomes a critical friend shadowing the steps set out in the rational planning model' (p. 38).",
    "She explains why the vision is so attractive to planners: it makes them the main actors, suggests planning is under their control, displays their knowledge, and presents itself as rational and therefore hard to argue with (p. 38). Failures are fixed inside the process, often with new tools such as GIS or ICT-supported engagement. Even when stakeholders appear, it is to garner support, identify values or find barriers. What is missing, implicitly, is the context outside the planning process: markets, power, politics. That is what later chapters of her book (and later weeks of the course) add."

   "h": "Connecting it to the course",
   "p": [
    "This section is a synthesis for the course, not Rydin's own claims. On the course dimensions, the governmental model sits at the universal, monist and realist end of the knowledge questions: knowledge is gathered 'in a positive way', experts are the authorised producers, and reality is knowable (as the lecture slide on the framework showed). It is strongly deliberate rather than emergent (outcomes follow from plans), leans towards agency of the planner rather than structure, and is reformist within existing institutions rather than transformative. Its normative commitment is the public interest understood as net welfare.",
    "Contrasts: Scott (same week) shows what happens when this model is backed by an authoritarian state and a high-modernist ideology; Davoudi (2006) questions the evidence-based part of it; Alexander argues rationality should be kept but made contingent. The rational choice lecture and Olson/Needham offer a different economic lens: instead of a benevolent planner correcting market failure, they model self-interested actors, including in government, and property rights. A useful exam habit: for any thesis, check for the signals the lecture listed: problem-solving language, instrumental questions, the planner as the case, and no questioning of the goals."
