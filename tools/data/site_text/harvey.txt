   "h": "What the article asks and why",
   "p": [
    "Harvey opens by placing this paper inside his long-running project: understanding how urbanisation shapes social change under capitalism. His starting point is dialectical. Cities are shaped by the logic of capital circulation and accumulation, but what gets built (roads, office districts, institutions) then constrains what capitalists and everyone else can do later. Capitalists, like workers, find themselves dominated and constrained by their own creations (p. 3). He also complains that studies of economic and social change too often treat urbanisation as a side-show. This paper tries to correct that by asking what role the urban process plays in the radical economic restructuring and uneven geographical development of the 1970s and 1980s (pp. 3–4).",
    "The concrete puzzle is a shift in how cities are governed. The abstract states it plainly: urban governance has become 'increasingly preoccupied with the exploration of new ways in which to foster and encourage local development and employment growth' (p. 3), whereas earlier decades focused on the local provision of services, facilities and benefits. Harvey calls the first stance entrepreneurialism and the second managerialism. He wants to know why this shift happened, how inter-urban competition shapes its outcomes, and what its macro-economic consequences are. His position in the debate is Marxist political economy, but, as the ending shows, not a purely pessimistic one."

   "h": "The shift from managerialism to entrepreneurialism",
   "p": [
    "Harvey's first piece of evidence is a colloquium in Orleans in 1985, which brought together academics, business people and policymakers from eight large cities in seven advanced capitalist countries (p. 4). There was a strong consensus that urban governments had to become much more innovative and entrepreneurial; the only disagreement was about how. Should cities create or rescue firms themselves, or confine themselves to offering infrastructure, sites, tax baits and cultural attractions? For Harvey this is symptomatic of a wider reorientation: the 'managerial' approach of the 1960s gave way to 'entrepreneurial' forms of action in the 1970s and 1980s, and remarkably the consensus seemed to hold across national boundaries and even across parties and ideologies (p. 4).",
    "He illustrates this from Britain and the United States. British local authorities became directly involved in economic development from the early 1970s, encouraged by central government; even David Blunkett, Labour leader in Sheffield, endorsed a kind of local entrepreneurship (p. 4). In the US, where boosterism (aggressive local promotion) had a long tradition, the cut in federal redistributions after 1972, when President Nixon declared the urban crisis over, revived boosterism so strongly that state and local governments were described as 'the last entrepreneurs' (p. 4). The shift was not complete: New Orleans remained a 'ward' of the federal government, dependent on redistribution, and the record was full of failures as well as successes (pp. 4–5).",
    "Why did it happen? Harvey lists a backdrop rather than a single cause: the recession of 1973, deindustrialisation, seemingly 'structural' unemployment, fiscal austerity at national and local levels, and a rising tide of neoconservatism with its appeal to market rationality and privatisation (p. 5). The nation state's declining power to control multinational money flows also meant that investment increasingly took the form of a negotiation between international finance and local powers trying to make their site attractive. He suggests that urban entrepreneurialism may have helped the transition from a Fordist-Keynesian regime of accumulation (mass production backed by the welfare state) to 'flexible accumulation' (more mobile, market-based production). He also warns that if cities are locked in zero-sum competition, even radical 'municipal socialists' will end up playing the capitalist game (p. 5)."

   "h": "Conceptual groundwork: process, things and governance",
   "p": [
    "Before going further, Harvey clears up two conceptual problems. The first is reification: talking about 'cities' as if they act. That language makes it seem as if cities are active agents when they are really things (p. 5). Urbanisation should rather be seen as 'a spatially grounded social process' in which many actors with different goals interact. Under capitalism the class practices linked to capital circulation and the reproduction of labour are hegemonic, although spatial practices also have gender, racial and bureaucratic contents. The process produces artefacts (built form, institutions, legal and administrative systems) that then dominate later action, so there is a permanent tension between process and thing (pp. 5–6). He adds that the shift must be studied at several scales, from neighbourhood to nation state (p. 6).",
    "The second point is that urban 'governance' means much more than urban 'government' (p. 6). The real power to reorganise urban life often lies in a broader coalition in which local government only facilitates and coordinates. Harvey lists the usual members: chambers of commerce, financiers, industrialists, property developers (the 'growth machine' in Molotch's term), but also universities, religious institutions, unions, parties, social movements and the many parts of the local state. Building such coalitions is delicate, which opens space for individuals with vision: a charismatic mayor such as Schaeffer in Baltimore, or private entrepreneurs in Halifax or Gateshead (pp. 6–7). So entrepreneurialism is not simply what city hall decides; it depends on class alliance formation."

   "h": "Three features of the new entrepreneurialism",
   "p": [
    "Using Baltimore as his main case, Harvey makes three broad assertions (p. 7). First, the centrepiece is the 'public-private partnership': traditional local boosterism combined with local government powers to attract external funding, direct investment and jobs. In Baltimore the turning point can be dated exactly: a narrowly passed 1978 referendum allowed city land to be used for the private development that became Harborplace, after which partnership had a popular mandate. Harvey notes that in the 1960s US cities had instead used redistribution (housing, education, health care for the poor) to regain social control after urban unrest, and that the role of the local state as facilitator of capitalist development had then declined (p. 7).",
    "Second, the partnership is entrepreneurial because it is speculative in design and execution, with all the dangers that brings compared with rationally planned development. Often 'the public sector assumes the risk and the private sector takes the benefits' (p. 7), though not always (the private risk taken in Gateshead's Metrocenter shows the exception). What is new, Harvey suspects, is risk absorption by the local rather than the national public sector. Third, entrepreneurialism focuses on the political economy of place rather than territory (pp. 7–8). Territorial projects such as housing and education improve conditions within a jurisdiction. Place projects (a civic centre, an industrial park, a waterfront) have indirect benefits that may be wider or narrower than the jurisdiction, and they tend to divert attention and resources from the region's broader problems. Baltimore's harbour could benefit the metropolitan region; New York's South Street Seaport mainly served local developers and financiers (p. 8)."

   "h": "Four strategies for competing cities",
   "p": [
    "Harvey then describes four basic options, which cities combine (pp. 8–10). First, competing in the international division of labour: creating or exploiting advantages for producing goods and services, such as resources, location, infrastructure, subsidies, labour quality and cost, and agglomeration economies (benefits of clustering many activities close together), as in Bologna and Emilia Romagna. Local wage control is easier when local bargaining replaces national bargaining. Second, competing in the spatial division of consumption: tourism, retirement, gentrification, cultural innovation, postmodern design, stadia, convention centres, marinas and spectacles. 'Above all, the city has to appear as an innovative, exciting, creative, and safe place to live or to visit, to play and consume in' (p. 9). Baltimore expanded tourist employment from under one to over fifteen thousand jobs in less than two decades.",
    "Third, cities compete for control and command functions in finance, government and information processing, which need expensive infrastructure such as airports, teleports and well-linked offices, and where established centres like New York and London have strong monopoly power (pp. 9–10). Fourth, they compete for redistributions from central (or US state) government, for example military contracts that sustained Bristol and Long Beach-San Diego (p. 10). The strategies are not exclusive, and uneven growth comes partly from synergy between them; Los Angeles grew through the interaction of defence spending, command functions and consumption activities (p. 10).",
    "This competition has a disciplining effect. As inter-urban competition becomes more potent it acts as an 'external coercive power' over individual cities, bringing them into line with the logic of capitalist development and forcing 'serial reproduction' of the same forms: world trade centres, waterfronts, cultural centres, postmodern shopping malls (p. 10). Falling transport costs make small differences between places (labour, infrastructure, taxation, regulation) matter more to mobile capital, so urban governance becomes oriented to a 'good business climate' (p. 11). Speculation follows because no one can predict which package will succeed, producing upward and downward spirals that leave the urban system more, not less, vulnerable (p. 11)."

   "p": [
    "Section 4 asks what all this means beyond individual cities (pp. 11–14). Paradoxically, public risk absorption and public infrastructure provision lower the cost of relocating for multinational firms, making them more rather than less mobile. Localities become sites for regulating labour relations, supporting flexible management strategies. Harvey therefore argues that urban entrepreneurialism facilitated the move from rigid Fordism to flexible accumulation, and links it to postmodern design: 'urban fragments' rather than comprehensive planning, ephemerality, and 'image over substance' (pp. 12–13).",
    "The distributive effects are regressive. Much of the US public-private partnership amounts to a subsidy for affluent consumers, corporations and command functions to stay in town, at the expense of collective consumption for the working class and poor (p. 12). Jobs polarise into low-paid and very high-paid positions, and informal work grows. Even progressive councils struggle, because competition works 'not as a beneficial hidden hand, but as an external coercive law forcing the lowest common denominator of social responsibility and welfare provision' (p. 12). Imitation makes any advantage ephemeral: how many convention centres and harbour places can there be? Instability follows: Houston, Dallas and Denver collapsed after 1980, New Orleans' World Fair was a disaster, credit-financed malls and stadia feed overaccumulation, and several 'festival market places' lost millions (p. 13).",
    "Why do such projects stay attractive? Because selling the city depends on an attractive urban image, and image has internal political uses (pp. 13–14). It counters alienation, creates civic pride and a sense of belonging, and so opens mechanisms of social control: the Roman formula of bread and circuses reinvented. Baltimore is the case in point: the harbour earned it the title of renaissance city while local studies found serious decay behind the glitter and accelerating decline. The image consolidated the power of the public-private partnership that built it. Harvey's verdict: 'The circus succeeds even if the bread is lacking. The triumph of image over substance is complete' (p. 14)."

   "h": "Autonomy, competition and the critical perspective",
   "p": [
    "In the final section Harvey addresses a theoretical debate on the 'relative autonomy' of the local state (pp. 14–15). Entrepreneurialism looks like considerable local autonomy, and he explicitly does not claim that urban governance is automatically captive to capitalist class interests. The key is timing. Following Marx, competition is the 'bearer' of capitalist social relations: its coercive laws discipline firms, states and cities, but the forcing occurs after the event rather than before (p. 15). Cities, like firms, experiment speculatively, and inter-urban competition then sorts winners from losers. Relative autonomy in this sense is fully consistent with capital accumulation. The managerial era of the welfare state and Keynesian compromise was the exception; the revival of civic boosterism, a tradition going back to the Hanseatic League, moves urban governance closer to the naked requirements of capital accumulation (p. 15).",
    "A critical perspective should therefore contrast the surface vigour of regeneration projects with underlying trends, notably a 'dual city' of inner-city regeneration surrounded by a sea of impoverishment, and should expose regressive distribution, volatility and ephemeral benefits (p. 16). Yet Harvey sees something positive too: the idea of the city as a collective corporation with democratic decision-making, with the Paris Commune as the paradigm case. The problem is less boosterism itself than the generality of inter-urban competition, which makes 'bad' projects drive out 'good' and forces well-meaning coalitions to be 'realistic' and 'pragmatic' (p. 16)."
