    "h": "What the chapters ask and why",
    "p": [
     "The mandatory reading is the first two chapters of Richard de Satgé and Vanessa Watson's book Urban Planning in the Global South (2018). Chapter 1 introduces the book and its Cape Town case; chapter 2 builds the theoretical frame. The opening questions set the tone: how different does planning theory and practice need to be in different parts of the world, and how far must planners understand the context in which they intervene? The authors argue that both theory and practice have favoured concepts that are 'place-blind' and held to be valid everywhere, and they want to 'foreground the importance of recognising place and location in planning' if planning is to reach its own aims of justice, equity and sustainability and avoid unintended harm (p. 1).",
     "The authors write from long experience in South Africa: Watson as a planning theorist, de Satgé as a researcher on land, livelihoods and migration in southern Africa, whose doctoral research forms the book's case. Their position is explicitly part of what they call the 'southern turn' in planning scholarship (p. 2). They add that imported concepts now come not only from the global North but also from rapidly modernising parts of the global South, and that this experience of watching ideas arrive from elsewhere, often with little thought about local conditions, motivated the book."

    "h": "The southern turn and the problem of 'landing' best practices",
    "p": [
     "Chapter 1 explains the southern turn briefly. Planning theory has long been built on the assumed characteristics of global North regions: relatively strong civil societies, well-resourced governance institutions, developed formal economies and lower poverty and unemployment (p. 2). Southern theorists argue that the global South differs enough to need new concepts, and is still shaped by colonial histories that tie it to the North, including in how knowledge is produced. Typical features include rapid urbanisation (though no longer in Latin America), weak and fractured civil society, poorly resourced institutions, largely informal economies and high poverty and inequality. Crucially, 'southern' refers less to a geographical South than to 'a perspective or orientation' (p. 2).",
     "The authors are especially concerned with how planning concepts travel. Approaches from the global North, and more recently from fast-modernising parts of the South, 'tend to ‘land’ in this part of the global South with little preliminary investigation of their potential ‘fit’, little consultation with those who feel their impact and frequently with highly negative outcomes' (p. 2). South Africa is a stark example: the apartheid government used Northern 'best practice' models such as Garden Cities, neighbourhood units, Radburn layouts and satellite towns to justify racial segregation and the forced removal of families of colour to distant new suburbs (p. 3). Apartheid has gone, but state planning still draws on inappropriate models that deepen the marginalisation of the poorest, mostly black, households."

    "h": "From an inquiry report to 'conflicting rationalities'",
    "p": [
     "The book's core concept has a concrete origin. In 2003 Watson read the report of a Commission of Enquiry set up by the Cape Town municipality after an informal settlement upgrade plan was rejected by the community and led to protests. The verbatim evidence showed a clash between the modernising ambitions of the state and the very different world views of shack dwellers, which were themselves fragmented and conflictual (p. 3). Watson (2003) coined 'conflicting rationalities' for this divergence.",
     "Her article also challenged the dominant planning theory of the time: communicative and collaborative planning, inspired by Habermas, which held that the right kind of dialogue between planners and communities could produce consensus. Instead, planners 'needed to understand how to accept and work with deep difference and ongoing conflict' (p. 4). The authors admit that a concept based on one case and secondary sources is hard to defend and risks oversimplifying state-society relations. The book therefore tests it in depth. Interestingly, interviews showed that officials did not consciously follow planning theories; they knew engagement should happen but found it too daunting, and higher-level officials discouraged it on the grounds that a democratic government 'knows what the people want' (p. 4)."

    "h": "Method and the Langa case",
    "p": [
     "The research is a single case study classed as 'paradigmatic' (following Flyvbjerg): a case with metaphorical and prototypical value that highlights general features of a society (p. 4). It is 'retroductive': it starts from a hypothesised concept (conflicting rationalities), goes to the empirical case, and then speaks back to the concept. The case is Langa, Cape Town's oldest African township, designed in 1923 along British Garden City lines, with about 52,000 people in 3.09 km2 in a city of 3.7 million (pp. 3, 5). Methods embraced the 'visual turn': satellite images and photographs served as triggers for storytelling by residents, officials and civil society actors, in two clusters of interviews (residents; strategic state actors and intermediaries). Interviews were held in English and Xhosa, which revealed gaps and distortions in meaning caused by translation (p. 5).",
     "The planning intervention studied is the N2 Gateway. In 2004 national housing policy was revised as Breaking New Ground (BNG), which sought well-located land for affordable, denser rental housing and in situ upgrading, reversing the first post-apartheid wave of peripheral housing that had reproduced apartheid geography. Yet BNG contained a contradiction: it announced a new upgrading instrument whose purpose was 'to support the focused eradication of informal settlements' (p. 6). The N2 Gateway megaproject (2005) piloted BNG in Langa and targeted Joe Slovo, an informal settlement along the N2 highway, aiming to 'eradicate' it and replace it with rental and ownership units (p. 6).",
     "The project sparked long conflicts. It escalated rival claims on space, identity and belonging between Langa 'borners' (people born in the city with pre-democratic urban residence rights), backyarders, hostel dwellers and informal settlers, and provoked protest, eviction attempts and a Constitutional Court judgement (p. 6). The conceptual frame draws on Foucault (power, governmentality, biopolitics) and on Tania Li's contrast between 'the will to govern and improve' and the poor's 'will to survive and thrive' (pp. 6–7)."

    "h": "What the book's later chapters find (as previewed)",
    "p": [
     "Chapter 1 previews the findings. Officials' narratives show that it is a fallacy to treat the state as a single entity: there are many wills to govern, diverse improvement agendas and conflicts within the state (p. 7). Residents' narratives show a granular mix of competing claims within the township, which the state's neat megaproject logic completely failed to read (p. 7). The concept of conflicting rationalities thus has value but needs elaboration in context, and the case challenges simple binaries of state versus society.",
     "Two implications follow for planning (p. 8). First, planners must understand contexts in depth before intervening, cataloguing the rationalities behind the will to govern and how they meet the will to survive: 'place' matters everywhere, but especially in under-researched regions or regions studied through imported categories. Second, the thick social complexity of Langa contrasts sharply with 'thin and instrumental assumptions' of public participation and community-driven collaborative planning found both in developmental-state discourse and in Northern planning theory. The book ends (chapter 8) by generating theoretical propositions to be tested elsewhere, not by claiming general validity."

    "h": "Chapter 2: the southern turn across disciplines",
    "p": [
     "Chapter 2 frames the whole book. It first reviews the southern turn more broadly. Postcolonial thought links power and knowledge: universal theory, when unpicked, often turns out to come from very specific and parochial situations (p. 12). The Comaroffs reject seeing the South as a source of 'unprocessed data'; Connell describes a global 'political economy of knowledge' in which the North controls funding, publishing and citation, so that Southern work is labelled mere data collection; Mignolo argues there is 'no modernity without coloniality' (p. 13). Even the IPCC has had 80–82% of its authors and reviewers from OECD countries (p. 14).",
     "In urban studies the debate is sharp. Scott and Storper argue that one general theory fits all cities and differences are just 'empirical variation'; Roy responds that this decontextualises cities and reproduces Eurocentrism, and that southern theory asks for 'a new relationality of theory', not just more city types (p. 14). Robinson's critique of 'global cities' and Roy and Ong's 'worlding' question best-practice copying (pp. 14–15).",
     "In planning, theory relies on Northern disciplines and on Habermas and Rawls, reflecting faith in Western liberal democracy. As Porter puts it, planning 'is not a view from nowhere' (p. 15). Planning differs from other disciplines because it must act, so bad theory has direct effects: African cities are littered with failed imported planning efforts based on wrong assumptions about household strategies, car ownership, land and institutional capacity (p. 16). Meanwhile Northern theory offers little help with deep conflict, profit-driven land development under outdated master plans, or patronage politics. Newer Southern work includes Holston's 'insurgent citizenship', Roy's and Yiftachel's reframing of informality as a practice of the state, Yiftachel's 'gray spaces', Bhan's analysis of Delhi courts using the 'public interest' to justify evictions, and Porter's point that categories such as state, civil society and property are assumed universal but harm those who cannot conform (pp. 17–19)."

    "h": "Why a 'southern theorising project'",
    "p": [
     "The authors prefer to speak of a 'southern theorising project' rather than a finished southern theory, and address four objections (pp. 20–24). First, does it create a new North/South binary? No: following Connell, the Comaroffs and Bhan, 'southern' is relational and perspectival, echoing Nietzschean perspectivism and Haraway's critique of 'the view from nowhere'; 'seeing from the South' exposes the parochialism of pseudo-universal theory (pp. 20–21). Second, can theory still generalise? Yes, but at a 'meso-level': midlevel concepts developed inductively, tied to the contexts that produced them, overlapping and changing over time, a position between universalism and particularism (pp. 21–22).",
     "Third, which conceptual lenses? Most (Marxist, Foucauldian, Habermasian) come from the North, but rejecting them leads nowhere; better is a 'critical appropriation' of existing knowledge, such as Chatterjee's critical use of Foucault (pp. 22–23). Fourth, which norms? Planning needs a normative stance, but values differ between and within places: informality, which people use to secure shelter and income, is seen by the wealthy and city managers as disorder, and reconciling such norms will take more than collaborative planning (p. 23). Instead of copying best practices, the authors endorse Healey's 'origin narratives' (every travelling idea should come with a story of its context and preconditions) and Flyvbjerg's phronetic, value-aware case research (pp. 23–24). In sum, the southern project is critical of existing theory, rooted in place, open to meso-level generalisation, and 'a conceptual rather than a physical location' (p. 24)."

    "h": "Conflicting rationalities: the concept",
    "p": [
     "The second half of chapter 2 defines the concept, posed as an alternative to consensus-based communicative and collaborative planning (p. 25). Rationality means a 'way of seeing', a perspective, a set of values or a world view of actors in a setting. Following Foucault and Flyvbjerg and Richardson, the authors accept that 'power is used (by actors in a particular context) to define what is counted as rationality and so what is counted as reality' (p. 26). Power is pervasive but can be productive as well as destructive, and framing knowledge as 'technical' or 'expert' is itself an exercise of power.",
     "Identity shapes rationality: ethnicity, race, class, gender and language all mattered in Cape Town, with Fincher and Jacobs' 'located politics of difference' showing that people mobilise different aspects of identity depending on context (pp. 26–27). Language is a key filter: English dominates policy and law, so officials who speak only English or Afrikaans cannot enter the Xhosa-speaking worlds of hostels and informal settlements unguided (pp. 27–28). Governmentality (Foucault's 'conduct of conduct') helps analyse who governs whom and how, and some postcolonial states show a 'shadow state' of brokers and contractors around the official state (p. 28).",
     "The central tension is between 'techno-managerial, modernising and marketised' state planning, often allied with profit-driven developers, and marginalised populations surviving largely under informality or 'illegality' (p. 29). It plays out at the 'interface' (Li): the times and places where rationalities meet and are contested, where issues are 'rendered technical' or where the marginalised may win gains. The authors immediately list critiques of their own concept: is it a simplistic binary? What about conflicts within groups, and actors who cross the interface? Can the encounter be positive? Any concept, they say, is a proposition to be tested (p. 29)."

    "h": "Conclusions and what the authors want",
    "p": [
     "The chapter's conclusion states the proposition the book tests: state-society engagement in planning in global South cities 'is shaped more often by a deep ‘conflict of rationalities’ between state and market, and impoverished urban communities, than by some kind of ‘public interest’ which could provide a starting point for participatory and consensus-seeking processes' (p. 30). What the authors want is not a new universal model, but planning that begins with deep contextual understanding, is honest about power and conflict, treats imported ideas with suspicion, and builds theory from Southern cases that can speak back to planning thought everywhere. Their own limits are acknowledged: one case, a provisional concept, and the risk of binary thinking."
