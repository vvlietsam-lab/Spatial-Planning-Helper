   "p": [
    "The file is the author's manuscript, so page numbers below refer to the manuscript (MS); the course's own quote from p. 56 of the journal corresponds to MS p. 9. Rydin starts from what Latour calls the breakdown of the modernist consensus (MS p. 2). In that consensus knowledge was a mirror of nature: an entity produced by experts in institutions that guaranteed its objectivity. Its breakdown means recognising that knowledge is constructed through social processes, that knowledge institutions are not necessarily neutral, and that knowledge is embedded in social relations and networks, such as epistemic communities (Haas) or communities of practice (Wenger). This matters for planning, which Sandercock describes as rooted in modernism: planners manage events for the public good, knowledge is harnessed for progress, and the planner's expert status rests on command of specialist knowledge (MS pp. 2–3).",
    "Her focus is institutional: what arrangements should planning processes have for handling knowledge (MS p. 3)? She argues that the usual answer from planning theory and science studies, deliberation, is inadequate, and proposes a pragmatic alternative: arenas where knowledge claims are tested and recognised. She first defines terms: “Knowledge differs from information and data in that the specification of a causal relationship is central to knowledge” (MS p. 4). Planning seeks specific impacts, so planners must understand how impacts follow from actions. Even a dataset relies on knowledge of such causal links to be used."

   "h": "From knowledge to knowledges",
   "p": [
    "After the modernist consensus, knowledge is no longer one category (Evans and Marvin): there are multiple claims to represent reality and multiple ways of knowing, held by many actors, not only experts (MS p. 4). Science studies stress lay knowledges (Wynne); environmental policy showed the value of local, experiential, contextualised knowledge compared to the non-local, objectified, generalised knowledge of science. Some warn against automatically prioritising local knowledge (Forsyth), but drawing on it is now accepted. Rydin separates this from calls for public debate on science, which aim to build trust in policy (MS p. 5).",
    "Planning theory made a parallel journey from modernism (peaking in 1970s systems theory) to a fragmented field in which a 'new orthodoxy' is emerging: planning as engaging stakeholders, giving them voice and seeking consensus (MS p. 5). Versions differ: consensus won by negotiation and mediation (Innes), consensus inherent in communication (Healey's collaborative planning), or empowerment of the most disadvantaged rather than consensus at any price (Sandercock). All challenge the planner as 'the knower'. Sandercock calls for an 'epistemology of multiplicity': knowing through dialogue, experience, local knowledge, reading symbolic and non-verbal evidence, and contemplative knowledge (MS p. 6). Planning's purpose becomes handling multiple knowledges, and the proposed method is deliberative and collaborative processes."

   "h": "The limits of deliberation for knowledge",
   "p": [
    "Some concerns about deliberation are general and well known (MS p. 7): vague guidance on practice, doubts about planners' skills, capture by powerful interests, and inability to resolve real conflicts of interest. Rydin adds concerns specific to knowledge. Deliberative processes are usually promoted to explore values and build trust, and an orientation towards agreement may not ensure that the most appropriate knowledge shapes decisions. Two questions follow: can these processes handle multiple knowledges, and can they distinguish knowledge from other bases for involvement?",
    "On the first: engaging lay and expert knowledges requires translation, but knowledges are not additive (Evans and Marvin), so a shared lingua franca does not resolve disagreement rooted in different experiences (MS p. 8). Innes herself says consensus building works through collective storytelling rather than the force of the better argument, and names two conditions: fully shared information, or situations where 'uncertainty is rampant'. In both, disputes are about values, either because knowledge is not contested or because no certain knowledge exists. Most planning lies in between. Hence: “engaging different knowledges is fundamentally different to engaging different voices” (MS p. 8).",
    "So Rydin recasts knowledge as knowledge claims: claims to understand causal relationships. These differ from ethical, pragmatic, efficiency or aesthetic claims. All matter, but they are different. Collins and Evans argue that stakeholder rights differ from rights based on expertise, and Habermas distinguishes validity claims to truth (constatives), normative legitimacy (regulatives) and truthfulness (expressives) (MS pp. 8–9). Rydin stresses that this is not a ranking: knowledge claims are not more important than ethical claims, just fundamentally different, and a planning process must be able to tell them apart."

   "h": "Why not every claim is knowledge",
   "p": [
    "Planning theory has blurred this distinction for two reasons (MS p. 9). It has focused on values and how to reach agreement between them; knowledge carries values but cannot be reduced to them. And it tends to label all community claims as knowledge, which is politically understandable because it raises their status. But, following Collins and Evans: “The experience of local stakeholders may be an important basis for claiming local or experiential knowledge but … such experience is not of itself a sufficient condition for it to be knowledge” (MS p. 9).",
    "Three examples make this concrete (MS pp. 9–10). With environmental risk, experts contrast their probabilistic assessment with community attitudes; local experience (for instance episodes of ill-health) may legitimately challenge expert assessments, but not every community perception of risk is knowledge. With cultural heritage, a community may hold historic knowledge of its area alongside emotional attachment and political claims based on its historic stake; these are distinct. A disadvantaged community can offer rich knowledge of lived experience that reveals overlooked poverty and shapes regeneration, and it also has a strong ethical claim to influence, but the ethical and knowledge claims do not coincide."

   "h": "Planners as co-producers: opening up and closing down",
   "p": [
    "Recognising knowledge claims also means recognising that planning co-produces knowledge (Jasanoff), in line with 'Mode 2' knowledge production in which practitioners and users take part (Gibbons et al.) (MS p. 10). Co-production has two parts: giving voice to actors with relevant knowledge claims, while recognising power differences, and actively recognising some claims as relevant knowledge, which requires testing them. Science studies make the same move: expertise is 'made' in debate but not all claims are equal (Liberatore and Funtowicz); Rip asks for assessment of robustness; Nowotny's 'socially robust knowledge' is tested for validity, involves an extended group of experts and is repeatedly tested and modified (MS p. 11).",
    "This fits collaborative theory's Habermasian roots (MS pp. 11–12). For Habermas only speech acts that carry criticisable validity claims constitute communicative action; consensus depends on convincing listeners that claims are rational, justified against appropriate criteria (facts, norms or authenticity), and a speech act must be both understandable (planning theory's emphasis) and warranted. White objects that people can understand without judging validity, but in planning, Rydin argues, actors should not accept claims on an unwarranted basis. Testing validity claims is an intuitive skill of competent speakers, ironically itself a product of modernity.",
    "So the planning system should be a series of arenas where knowledges engage, with planners co-generating knowledge by testing and recognising claims: opening-up (giving voice) and closing-down (testing and recognising). “Contemporary planning theory has tended to be better at discussing opening-up than closing-down” (MS p. 13). This matches a view of knowledge as socially constructed and engaged with material reality: 'realist constructivism' (Wynne). Arenas may require supporting actors without resources, and accepting that claims come in different languages; it is patronising to assume lay people can only offer experiential stories or emotions (MS p. 13)."

   "h": "A typology of knowledge claims",
   "p": [
    "Rydin starts from a generic concept of planning with a normative focus: improving the natural and built environment for society (MS p. 14). Figure 1: planning seeks to turn State A into B1; without planning, societal, economic and environmental processes would turn A into B; actual outcomes differ from intentions, so the result is B2. Planning is part of those societal processes, not outside them, and it has real effects, even if not the stated ones (against the view that planning is purely symbolic). Her housing example: B is the anticipated shortage, B1 the planned match of supply and need, B2 profitable developments alongside continuing overcrowding, housing stress and homelessness (MS p. 15).",
    "This yields seven types (MS pp. 15–17): (1) knowledge of current State A as a benchmark; (2) of predicted State B, since B1 must be judged against B, not A; (3) of processes moving A to B; (4) of how planning could move A to B1; (5) of actual outcomes B2; (6) of how A became B2; and (7) normative knowledge of desired State B1. The seventh is explicitly normative, but still knowledge because a goal must engage with possible realities: 'not any imaginings will serve'. All types share two features: none is value-free, since framing involves choices (describing an area by market prices or by child poverty makes a difference), and each carries an embedded causal story (MS p. 17)."

   "h": "Testing and recognising each type (Table 2)",
   "p": [
    "The task is to create arenas to engage, test and recognise claims, Rip's 'hybrid forums' (MS pp. 17–18). Table 2 collapses the seven types into four. Empirical claims come in lay and expert forms (hence 'experiential knowledge'); the planner acts as consultancy client, data analyst or facilitator of community input, and needs forums where expert meets expert, lay meets lay, and lay and expert examine each other's claims, with procedures to resolve them (MS p. 18). Process claims need theoretically framed research, sometimes action research, in which planners are object of or participants in research; testing means engaging rival causal models, in academia or policy evaluations, until the planner judges a model robust enough for decisions (MS p. 19).",
    "Predictive claims resemble process claims but are mostly expert-led, since lay knowledge is rooted in past and present; the planner is the client of researchers, alert to lay input, and testing adds debate on prediction techniques (MS p. 19). Normative claims belong in debate in the public sphere; academics can help by developing alternative scenarios (Flyvbjerg's phronesis). The planner manages informed debate, checks that proposed futures are genuinely possible and makes sure no possible future is ruled out, thereby adding 'an ethical voice of their own' (MS p. 20). Table 1 applies the typology to three examples: sustainable construction, where expert knowledge must be open to building users and NGOs; the housing market, where developers' framing of the 'problem' must be challenged; and landscape values, which mix expert and lay assessments (MS pp. 20–21). Without testing forums, knowledge claims lose their specific character and dissolve into other claims."

   "h": "Conclusions and what the author wants",
   "p": [
    "Rydin wants to bridge modernist and postmodern planning theory (MS p. 21). Knowledge is socially constructed, multiple and made of claims open to contestation and recognition, yet knowledge has a specific contribution that must not be lost. Planning should hear many voices for the sake of democracy and empowerment, but also create specific spaces to test knowledge claims. “Not all claims within planning can be recast as knowledge claims just to promote the status of the claim-maker” (MS pp. 21–22). The value of knowledge must be asserted alongside the value of hearing diverse stakeholders. Her caveats are that the typology simplifies, that she deliberately leaves the planning approach open beyond a generic normative focus, and that the institutional design of forums is sketched rather than worked out."
