     "Witte (Utrecht University, who also gives lectures 4 and 5), Wiegmans (Municipality of Purmerend) and Louw (TU Delft) study a very Dutch problem: ports that sit inside dense urban regions and have almost no room left. Traditionally, port geography looked at the port-city relation mainly through economic efficiency: how can the port handle more cargo? The authors argue that this is too narrow. Next to port growth, land is now claimed by the energy transition, the circular economy, climate adaptation, reducing urban heat stress, water quality, external safety and housing (p. 1).",
     "Their central finding is in the title and abstract: 'even though the net claims do not yet outnumber the available land, the cumulative effects of these diverging interests do, which suggests that the battle for space is about to intensify as there are more claims than land' (p. 1). The research question is: 'How to identify and mitigate land-use conflicts in the port-city interface in densely populated areas?' (p. 2). The 'port-city interface' is the zone where port functions and urban functions meet and compete for the same space."

    "h": "Why the port can no longer simply move away",
    "p": [
     "The introduction sets out two perspectives on competition for space. From the urban side (city-port), pressure comes from housing demand and densification policies that push into port areas; Amsterdam's Haven-Stad project, which plans large-scale housing along the IJ banks, is the main example.      "For a long time such conflicts could be 'avoided' by moving industrial port activities to peripheral sites downstream. The authors state that 'This ‘fleeing’ of the port is no longer a viable option', because whole port regions are now intertwined with urban land uses (p. 2). Co-existence of port and city within the existing space becomes the norm, and with it land-use conflict. To capture this, they propose a multi-facetted view based on Campbell's sustainability triangle (cited in its 2016 version), considering regional-economic, urban and socio-ecological development together, and giving room to the full range of stakeholders, including the local port community (p. 2)."

    "h": "Three spatial levels of the port-city",
    "p": [
     "The literature review first traces how the port-city concept evolved. It began with transport flows at quays, and Bird's (1963) 'Anyport' model described six phases in which new facilities were built ever further downstream. In the last decade attention shifted to planning, governance and actor behaviour. Most models came from Western ports; Middle-Eastern and Asian ports kept closer, more reciprocal port-city relations (p. 2). The authors distil three levels.",
     "The highest level is the port region: studies of polycentric port regions and multi-port gateways, of connectivity and of entrepreneurship show growing awareness of competing and overlapping land uses (p. 2). The middle level is the port-city interface itself. Bird, Hoyle and Norcliffe described a growing separation of port and city, which made sense when space was abundant; now it is not, so they 'increasingly compete for the same space' (p. 2). Wiegmans and Louw (2011) added port regulation (environment, transport, spatial planning, labour, safety), widening the concept beyond economics (p. 2).",
     "The lowest level is the waterfront. Daamen's study of Rotterdam and Hamburg found that planners now propose redeveloping port areas still in use, and that city and port are engaged in 'a similar battle to attract people and business' (p. 3). Charlier warned that 'residual maritime potential' is 'non-renewable': once port land becomes city, port functions will 'never' return (p. 3)."

    "p": [
     "Section 2.2 uses Campbell's three conflicts to show that port-city challenges are multi-dimensional (p. 3). The resource conflict (economy versus environment) appears in ports as sites of production and transshipment of building materials: good for jobs and agglomeration, but with pollution, congestion and emissions that hurt nature and residents; hence today's debate on circularity. The property conflict (economy versus equity) appears in the mix of public and private land ownership, which shapes whether areas can be redeveloped for housing and whether planners pursue integration (mixed uses) or separation.      "The development conflict (equity versus environment) means that just urban development in these districts must also be green, which raises development and living costs. This is hard because port-city districts are historically blue-collar; new, higher-value activities are unaffordable to existing residents and may displace them. Port areas are also vulnerable to sea level rise, and climate-adaptive, green redevelopment further increases gentrification. Space for water safety, offshore wind and hydrogen adds to the pressure (pp. 3–4)."
