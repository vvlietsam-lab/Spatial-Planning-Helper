   "p": [
    "Page numbers are journal pages (Environment and Planning B, 47(2), 203–219); the file is the online-first version (pp. 1–17), where journal page = online page + 202. Cozzolino opens with two epigraphs: Jacobs on Utopias where only the planners in charge may have significant plans, and Habraken's “If a built environment is a complex organism, it is so by virtue of human actions: people imbue it with life” (p. 203). The traditional city has not only aesthetic qualities but adaptive capacities, often contrasted with the rigidity of large residential neighbourhoods built all-at-once and from scratch in the 20th century, which he calls anti-adaptive neighbourhoods (AANs). While the traditional city works as an adaptive system, AANs are 'under a lock-in situation' that compromises their long-term survival (p. 204). His illustration is from his home town Bergamo: 'La Clementina' (1980s) versus the historic 'Borgo Santa Caterina', which keeps changing in small ways.",
    "AANs had real merits: they met great housing pressures, handled emergencies such as earthquakes and floods, and let families become owners at affordable prices (p. 204). But they show little openness to incremental development; built instantly, they remain largely unchanged. Social and economic change still happens inside them, but they rarely undergo spontaneous physical re-adaptation. Because they cannot self-regenerate, top-down interventions follow, creating 'questionable paternalistic circles' (p. 205). The article has two goals: “first, it aims at characterizing the causes behind the formation and perpetuation of AANs; second, it aims at providing policy and design implications” (p. 205). Its novelty is attention to three underestimated factors: “(i) action (as the main source of urban change), (ii) ownership (as the legal title that effectively distributes ‘design control’ in the built environment), and (iii) time” (p. 206). He is not nostalgic: the focus is 'the institutional framework that shaped its evolutionary process', which distances him from New Urbanism."

   "h": "Why adaptability matters",
   "p": [
    "Adaptive systems adjust to new conditions, learn from experience and can be modified for new purposes; anti-adaptive systems keep their structure whatever happens (p. 206). In the built environment adaptability matters for three reasons. Persistence: as Habraken says, 'continuous renewal and replacements preserves it', because users can change their surroundings as their needs, technologies and lifestyles change. Identity: adaptive environments allow places with unique characters to emerge. Innovation: adaptability makes efficient use of 'dispersed local knowledge' (Hayek 1945; Jacobs 1961), letting people renew the urban fabric in ways nobody could predict, so that society benefits from continuous trial and error. The article assumes that current debates can learn from the old principles and processes that shaped the traditional city."

   "h": "How the traditional city grew",
   "p": [
    "Following Pirenne, the European city's beauty goes back to the 11th-century civitas, where citizenship required owning a dwelling (p. 206). Citizens expressing themselves through their houses drove the city's growth; Romano speaks of the 'deliberate aesthetic will of their citizens'. This was not a legal vacuum: codes aimed at ensuring minimum damage to existing structures and fairness between neighbours (Hakim), so change was accepted if it did not compromise others' rights, and owners were otherwise free (pp. 206–207). Municipalities collected taxes for essential public works and services. The founding principles were private ownership, incremental development and organic adjustment among households, with two virtues: respect for the existing environment and openness to additions from many decisions (p. 207)."

   "h": "From proscriptive to prescriptive rules",
   "p": [
    "The old codes were mainly proscriptive: prohibitions ('thou shalt not' instead of 'thou shalt') that could not impose any predetermined spatial configuration and only prevented negative externalities on others' property, such as pollution or noise (p. 207). They therefore allowed spontaneous, unique settlements. Modern planning, aiming at coordinated and less disordered development, turned to prescriptions: individual actions were regulated positively to reach the goals of comprehensive land-use plans, and national governments imposed the same criteria on all cities. This lowered the spontaneity that had produced unique places. His test: if we tried to recreate traditional patterns today, standards on minimum road widths, building distances, parking and green space would largely prevent it (p. 207).",
    "AANs spread with modern planning (pp. 207–208). The idea was self-sufficient neighbourhood units fostering 'sane and morally intact urban life'; after the Second World War housing demand exploded and healthy dwellings were urgently needed. But AANs also reflected urban, social and political ideologies. The planner's scale of reference changed from designing public spaces and infrastructure to total design and comprehensive large-scale plans. What had been the incremental result of many citizens became the product of central decisions, strict prescriptions and master plans. Self-building became harder, streets were separated from buildings in favour of ample, undefined, underused open space, and small demand-driven developments gave way to large supply-driven ones (p. 208)."

   "h": "What makes a neighbourhood anti-adaptive",
   "p": [
    "AANs are everywhere; about 16% of Europeans live in large prefabricated estates, some publicly owned, some private, some public and later partly privatised, and new ones are still built (p. 208). Settlements range from complex and open to change to simple and reluctant to change, visible in the intensity of spontaneous actions over time. A simple configuration has a designed, artificial order; a complex one is emergent, the result of spontaneous action. This is a trade-off: “the more one neighbourhood is designed from the top, the less space there is for complexity to evolve” (p. 209). Adaptability is relative, a matter of degree, and not every characteristic must be present.",
    "Figure 3 lists eight characteristics (pp. 209–211), summarised in the table: attractiveness that fades over time; a large scale of design; a single designer or team; construction all at once; planning rules that coincide with the built configuration, freezing density, heights and uses; mono-functionality (the 'dormitory'); large open spaces managed by one agency, with Jacobs' problem of 'streets without eyes'; and communal ownership such as big condominiums, where changing anything needs large majorities, an 'anti-commons' problem. Adaptive environments have the opposite features: plot-scale design, as many designers as users over time, incremental construction that avoids synchronous decay, rules that leave room beyond what exists, mixed and changing uses, little open space, and many independent owners, because 'small groups adapt more quickly than large groups' (Brand)."

   "h": "Planning's share of the responsibility",
   "p": [
    "Cozzolino is careful about blame (p. 212). Planning has direct responsibility for three characteristics. It pushes large-scale developments, using plans less to regulate small private actions than to attract politically strategic projects. It demands large public open spaces through standards on minimum unbuilt area. And it produces rules that freeze adaptation, since the room for change depends 'also and even mostly' on the space rules leave for future actions. For the other characteristics its role is indirect: large-scale development brings centralised design control, all-at-once construction and collectively managed buildings and spaces. Functions and attractiveness can only be encouraged: planning cannot force people to run businesses in an AAN or to visit it, and the state cannot supply all activities that make a neighbourhood vibrant (p. 212)."

   "h": "What planners should do",
   "p": [
    "General principles (pp. 212–213). Include time: the main difference between traditional and modern environments is the role time played in forming them; Akbar asks why we analyse the end-product rather than the process that produced it. This does not mean building slowly, but leaving room after construction for progressive, unpredictable adaptation, moving from a static architectural view to one of change and self-organising complexity, for example when a flat becomes an office or a storey is added. Such actions rest on 'locality knowledge' (Jacobs): people's subjective understanding of their own situation, needs and means. The challenge is to let people use it while avoiding undesirable externalities; Oosterwold is cited as an experiment. And welcome multiple independent ownerships and distributed design control, since, as Moudon says, “everything depends on the pattern of ownership” (p. 213).",
    "For existing AANs (pp. 213–214), abandon paternalism; Jacobs: 'we must regard dwellers as people capable of understanding and acting upon their own self-interests'. Enlarge room for local experimentation: allow demolition and rebuilding, extra floor area for densification, and a plurality of uses. Rule changes alone may fail, because communal ownership can stifle self-regeneration. Rules should not impose 'a stringent technocratic order', since future needs cannot be predicted; when rules simply copy the initial design, the knowledge embedded in continual neighbouring adaptation is lost. Planners can also improve accessibility and connections, abandoning the ideal of self-sufficient units.",
    "For new neighbourhoods (pp. 214–215), embrace 'real' complexity. Participatory design and city gaming are welcome but still tend to produce rational, mechanical designs fixed in space: more complex designs, not spaces where self-organising complexity can emerge. Copying the traditional neighbourhood's form cannot reproduce its complexity. Favour demand-driven development and many builders of various sizes, abandon the political use of large projects that centralise design control and favour big developers, and promote self-building (only about 10–15% of new homes even in the Netherlands and UK in 2015). Separate publicly relevant assets from private space: public space as 'a robust skeleton' designed top-down, with bottom-up design on individual lots; public spaces only when strictly necessary. A note adds that total spontaneity (favelas, slums) may not produce good settlements and that some top-down design may be indispensable (p. 216)."

   "h": "Conclusions and what the author wants",
   "p": [
    "Cozzolino stresses that he does not blame or condemn AANs as such; they matter to the many people living in them (p. 215). The challenge is creating conditions for more adaptive environments, by paying attention to action, ownership and time. His core claim: “the main problem with AANs is not so much, as many planners and policy makers largely believe, the need for further centrally coordinated plans, investments, and interventions, but rather the lack of flexible space for incremental adaptation and the evolution of self-organized orders” (p. 215). He lists open questions: the micro-dynamics of AANs; whether design solutions can combine the benefits of common ownership (lower prices, shared services, density) with the freedom of many dwellers; and whether preserving historic quarters, which can themselves become anti-adaptive through restrictive rules, is compatible with long-term adaptability (pp. 215–216)."
