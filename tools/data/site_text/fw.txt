   "h": "What the chapter asks and why",
   "p": [
    "Rydin opens Theory in Planning Research (2021) with a problem every new planning student meets: planning research is extremely varied, with many topics, questions and theories. Unlike economics, where micro- and macro-economic theory dominates and 'alternative' frameworks are only promoted in certain places, one planning school can use very different theoretical reference points. Researchers therefore face a choice: “How is that choice to be made? What is at stake?” (p. 2). The book answers by showing how theory guides and frames research, and what choosing one framework over another means for the research questions, the methodology, the way results are presented and the findings. Each later chapter outlines one perspective and then discusses published studies that used it.",
    "Rydin sets five caveats (pp. 2–3). First, this is not a book of planning theory. Planning theory, in her usage, is normative: it asks how planning should be done. Her book is analytic: it offers frameworks to understand how planning processes work. Recommendations may follow, but only after the analysis. She immediately adds that the two cannot be fully separated: “all research—including analytic research—is imbued with value judgements” (p. 2). Second, the selection reflects her own knowledge; feminism, for instance, is folded into urban politics (ch. 6) and postcolonial approaches into political economy (ch. 7). Third, she deals with theory of planning, not theory for planning (Faludi 1973). Fourth, the book concerns empirical, data-based social science. Fifth, it is not a methods textbook."

   "h": "Defining 'planning' is already a theoretical choice",
   "p": [
    "Before discussing theory, Rydin asks what planning research studies. You might call your work planning research because you sit in a planning school or study people called planners, but any definition “implies a framing of that object of research and that has theoretical implications” (p. 4). She shows three small shifts. Planning as goal-directed decision-making frames it as an instrumental process (means to ends) and a public administration activity, downplaying politics; Næss and Saglie (2000) take this line and, Rydin notes, accept planners' intentions at face value. Planning as the state ordering the environment in the public or collective interest reframes it as intervention that corrects imbalances and inefficiencies of market-led change. A politicised definition sees planning as engaging different interests, to be judged by who benefits, or even as maintaining (or not) systemic features of society (pp. 4–5).",
    "Each framing carries value judgements about what is important and what planning should do. That does not turn analytic research into normative planning theory, but it means analytic work can be aligned with or against professional and political ideologies (p. 5). Planning is also very diverse across systems, scales and domains. What the cases share is attention to the future and how it relates to current decisions, a focus on the natural and built environment with an eye to society, economy and environment, and a mix of political and technical work. Beyond that, Rydin refuses a generic definition: one purpose of theory is precisely to supply a more detailed and specific definition of planning for a given study (p. 5)."

   "h": "Why do planning research, and why it is hard",
   "p": [
    "Research is more than collecting facts about planning; a doctorate requires a 'contribution to knowledge' (p. 6). Knowledge, for Rydin, goes beyond description: it means understanding the dynamics that produced a situation, 'a claim to know the cause of things' (the LSE motto rerum cognoscere causas). She warns that this motto can suggest a too linear link between cause and effect; later chapters offer other views of change. Research therefore combines a general understanding of how planning 'works' (or doesn't) with knowledge of one case, and moves in both directions: the case improves the general picture, and the general picture guides the case study (p. 6).",
    "Research is also social: a dialogue with other researchers, for which a shared theory is an entry point, and ideally with practitioners. Rydin takes seriously the complaint of a gap between academy and profession: planners in a survey wanted “real, tangible issues” rather than “creating a utopia” (Goodman et al., quoted p. 7), and Campbell et al. warn that constant critique can breed conservatism and inaction. She gives two responses (p. 7). Research led by a practice agenda carries that agenda's values; goals like sustainability, justice and economic development seem unassailable, yet planners must repeatedly choose between them, and denying this turns planning into a technical 'best practice' exercise. And theory-informed research can support practitioners' reflection on how processes were generated and suggest tentative pathways for change.",
    "Research is difficult (p. 8). Students worry about the amount and quality of data, subjectivity and getting beyond description, and must balance rigour against the pragmatism needed to finish at all. Crucially, data almost never proves a finding unequivocally: it has to be interpreted into a narrative, and the same data can support different narratives. Her example: data on housebuilding rates reads differently from a historical perspective, from a focus on housebuilders' behaviour, or from a record of planners' decisions and their rationale. Each narrative implies value judgements, so the researcher must build the most persuasive narrative the data allows, and an explicit theoretical perspective helps with exactly that."

   "h": "What theory is",
   "p": [
    "Rydin distinguishes two views of theory (p. 9). One sees theory as a law connecting variables, as in the natural sciences, tested by searching for confirming or refuting evidence. Following Flyvbjerg et al. (2012), she rejects this model for social science: the social world is complex and interacts with built and natural environments, and there is a 'double hermeneutic' (Giddens 1986): social scientists interpret the way people themselves interpret the world. The alternative she adopts sees theory as “a heuristic aid to understanding a situation, a way to try to know what is essentially unknowable in its completeness” (p. 9). A heuristic is a tool that helps you find things out without guaranteeing a complete answer. The world is irreducibly complex; we understand more by layering analyses and relating them to each other.",
    "Theory is therefore a narrative account of the world and of planning processes: “Theory is, therefore, best seen as a set of ideas that fit together coherently and make general statements about the world or a part of it” (p. 9). It is mostly text, sometimes supported by diagrams or formulas, and its persuasiveness, both in itself and in how it illuminates empirical research, is central. Theories are not arbitrary windows on the world. Each involves an ontology (assumptions about how the world is), normative positions (how it should be, even if unstated) and an epistemology (how knowledge can be generated through research). So the terrain of theory is contested, and choosing a theory means making value judgements part of your research, which Rydin calls 'often profoundly satisfying' (pp. 9–10)."

   "h": "Is theory necessary? Empiricism and phronesis",
   "p": [
    "Empiricism claims the facts can speak for themselves: collect data rigorously and let patterns emerge, since the world is 'out there' and just needs looking at. Latour called this the 'modernist settlement' (p. 10). Rydin's objections are pragmatic. Value judgements run through the supposedly neutral practice: you cannot collect all data, so why this data, collected this way, on a topic defined this way? Data also allows alternative interpretations, and choosing one again involves values. And pure empiricism produces a disparate field: each situation is researched in detail, but findings are hard to connect. Theory answers both problems, by focusing attention and by building a broader picture of planning (p. 10).",
    "She then discusses phronesis, Flyvbjerg et al.'s (2012) call for research as practical wisdom: situated knowledge about how to act in specific settings, based on deliberation about values and interests, drawing on local and tacit knowledge and aiming at praxis, change with an appreciation of 'the ineliminable presence of power' (p. 11). Phronetic research asks four questions: Where are we going with this problematic? Who gains and who loses, by which mechanisms of power? Is this desirable? What, if anything, should we do?",
    "Rydin raises two problems (p. 11). First, phronesis dismisses theory using a straw man of what theory can be, and presents good research as a virtue of the researcher's character; she prefers to see research as a skill that can be learnt. Second, phronesis makes theoretical judgements without admitting them. It relies on Giddens' double hermeneutic, which is a theoretical choice. Its praxis also implies theory: a researcher who works with political leaders and chief executives to change planning assumes change comes through feedback or communicative action (chs 2 and 5); one who helps community groups in planning disputes assumes planning is a political process 'riven with conflict' (ch. 6). Phronesis can be combined with theory, but it should go with an explicit theoretical framing."

   "h": "Three advantages of using theory",
   "p": [
    "Rydin lists three advantages (pp. 11–12). First, focus: frameworks differ in what they consider important, so the chosen theory makes these judgements explicit and lets the researcher concentrate on certain issues, data and sources and downplay others, which is essential in a project limited in time and funding. Second, connection: every empirical account is partial and provisional, and linking it to a theoretical account fits it into a broader, pre-existing story about planning. This helps analysis and conclusions, makes generalisation possible, and a persuasive theory reinforces a persuasive analytic narrative. Third, theory building: empirical research can build, revise and amplify frameworks, linking the particular and the general and contributing to a deeper understanding of society through the study of planning.",
    "Normative and analytic theory overlap (pp. 12–13). Governance theory (analytic, from political science) explains urban policy through networks of stakeholders; collaborative planning theory (normative) wants to improve planning through more communicative action between stakeholders, aiming at mutual understanding and possibly consensus. Both focus on stakeholder interaction, so one framework can have analytic and normative sides. Practitioners find theory's language unintelligible and its tone too critical and sweeping; Rydin remains optimistic that research can be translated and support self-reflection, but insists that “it is the role of the academy to do the best job it can of the research task first” (p. 13)."

   "h": "The map of the book: how each theory reacts to the governmental model",
   "p": [
    "The rest of the book forms an arc (pp. 13–14): from the ideal of planning as a linear rational activity (the governmental model, ch. 2), through actor-centred approaches that stress the agency of individuals and organisations (rational choice, new institutionalism, governance, urban politics; chs 3–6), to a structural account (Marxian political economy, ch. 7), and finally two relational perspectives (Foucault, assemblage and material entities; chs 8–9) that unsettle conventional ideas of agency and even of the actor. The approaches also differ in how they think planning can be improved (pp. 14–15). Governmental: better action by planners and officials, with politics setting goals. Rational choice: realign incentive structures. New institutionalism: influence organisational culture through learning. Governance: planners skilled in partnership working, managing networks and collaboration. These four 'look inward' at planning actors and organisations.",
    "The other approaches look beyond the state. Urban politics sees certain groups as repeatedly disadvantaged and wants them better resourced, listened to and given more power. Political economy also puts socio-economic inequality at the centre but looks to structural change in society. Foucauldian approaches tend towards the structural and their stress on hidden power suggests little prospect of improvement, though Foucault's idea of resistance offers a pathway. Relational approaches such as actor-network theory are criticised as detailed description rather than a theory of dynamics, so their prescriptions are small-scale experimentation and nuanced changes in assemblages (p. 15).",
    "Table 1.1 (p. 15) then shows each approach as a reaction to the limits of the governmental approach, which is closest to planning's self-presentation as a rational, expert-led process and offers a template that, if it fails, is only adjusted at the margin. Rydin deliberately does not add a limitations section per chapter: the chapters already criticise each other, all approaches can yield insight, and researchers “have a right to reflect their own value judgements in their research work” (p. 16). She admits being drawn to some perspectives more than others but tried not to let this shape her account. The example papers in later chapters are presented, not critiqued (p. 16)."

   "p": [
    "Rydin wants planning researchers to choose a theory consciously and to know what it can and cannot do for their research. Theory is not an optional decoration: even defining planning, selecting data and interpreting it involve theoretical and value choices, and a theory makes those choices explicit and defensible. She repeats near the end of the chapter that “all theoretical endeavours in the social sciences are inevitably value-laden, and a rigorous approach should seek to make this clear and explicit” (p. 14). The limits she admits are about scope: the selection of theories is partial and shaped by her interests, the book stays analytic rather than normative, it concerns empirical social science only, and it presents example studies without critiquing them.
