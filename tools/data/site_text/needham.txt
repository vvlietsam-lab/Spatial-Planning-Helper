  "Rights (pp. 26–28): ownership is never absolute; owners may not seriously harm others (though many countries allow harm if the benefit exceeds it, Coase), and owners can grant partial rights (a lease). The state protects interests in two ways: private law (the interest becomes a property right the holder enforces in court) and public law (a public authority such as a municipality restricts how others use their rights, e.g. via building permits).    "h": "What the chapter asks and why",
   "p": [
    "Needham's chapter opens a book on planning, law and property rights. It starts by rejecting a familiar way of thinking: that land-use planning opposes the market, restricts property rights, and is justified because a public authority must act 'in the public interest' (p. 23). Instead he looks at the interests that people have in how land is used. Some of these interests are protected by private law as property rights, which their holders can use in the market. Others are recognised by the state but not made into property rights; a public authority protects them on citizens' behalf through public law. That, for Needham, is what land-use planning is.",
    "The crucial point is that the line between interests protected as rights and interests protected by planning 'is not absolute and is the result of a political decision' (p. 23). So he asks: could some goals of land-use planning be reached by turning interests into rights, so that markets in those rights produce the desired land use? He is careful about the scope. The chapter investigates feasibility only; it 'does not recommend replacing land-use planning by markets in property rights' (p. 23). He also does not question the legitimacy of the state influencing land use, only the methods it uses (pp. 23–24)."

   "h": "Planning works by influencing private decisions",
   "p": [
    "Needham defines land-use planning as what public authorities do when they try to reach a politically chosen goal by deliberately influencing how land in an area is used: efficient production, safer traffic, affordable housing, protected habitats and so on (p. 23). The usual method is regulation: building and development need a permit, given only if the initiative fits a plan (p. 24). The alternative he explores is creating markets in property rights so that people's own initiatives automatically realise the policy goals. Possible advantages: citizens are freer and more responsible for their surroundings and can use rights to protect themselves against the powerful; the planning agency faces less direct involvement, less financial risk and less risk of freezing an inappropriate land use (p. 24).",
    "He then makes an observation that the lecture picked up. Achieving planning goals depends on how land is used, not who owns it, and under planning most land-use decisions are still taken voluntarily by owners. Planning is not really 'command and control'. The authority has no right to decide unless it owns the land. Hence: 'Land-use planning will succeed only if it can influence the private decisions' (p. 24). Tools for this influence include development control, subsidies and levies, persuasion, and providing infrastructure; only expropriation takes the decision away from the owner, typically for roads or open space (pp. 24–25)."

   "h": "From 'the public interest' to identifiable private interests",
   "p": [
    "Why would an authority want to influence land use at all? The standard answer is 'in the public interest', which Needham finds unsatisfactory (p. 25). It implies one use of land is more in the public interest than another, which is very hard to substantiate. Economic arguments about 'distortions' such as external effects and monopoly (the welfare economics behind Rydin's governmental model) no longer convince him either. His main objection is that 'the public interest' suggests you and I must be constrained for the benefit of 'the public', yet 'we too are the public!' (p. 25). Like economists' talk of 'social costs', it depersonalises the people affected into an amorphous 'society'.",
    "So he looks at real interests. As a landowner I care how my parcel is used, but also how my neighbour uses hers: I do not want her to undermine my foundations; I may not want her door painted bright pink; I care about traffic in my street, the vitality of local shops, an open landscape, clean air (p. 25). His reformulated answer: an authority influences land use because one person's decision about her land can affect another person's interests. It acts 'not so much to uphold some general public interest, as to uphold the identifiable interests of many identifiable private persons' (p. 26)."

   "h": "Property rights are created by the state, not natural",
   "p": [
    "The state protects interests in land in a second way too: by creating and protecting property rights, and the two ways often clash, which is why planning is politically contested (p. 26). Needham puts ownership in perspective. Property rights give security that helps markets work and stabilises social relations, as landlord and tenant law shows. But ownership is never absolute. The main restriction is that owners may not use land in ways that greatly harm others, although many countries allow harmful use if the benefit exceeds the harm (Coase 1960). A second restriction arises when an owner voluntarily grants a partial right, such as a lease, which courts will protect even against the owner (pp. 26–27).",
    "These restrictions operate between private persons: the harmed party, not the state, takes the initiative to go to court. Yet the state decides which rights it will enforce, so it must define them. A property right is therefore a way of using land that is 'recognized by, and protected by, the courts' (p. 27). This undermines the idea, found in Locke, that property rights are natural or inalienable. Needham quotes Bromley: 'It is the fact that something is protected that makes it a right, rather than that something is protected because it is a right' (p. 27). Different states can and do create different property rights."

   "h": "Two ways of protecting interests, and rights as a policy variable",
   "p": [
    "This yields his key distinction (pp. 27–28). The 'private law' way: the state declares an interest a property right, and the holder protects it herself through civil courts. The 'public law' way: the state empowers a public authority, such as a municipality, to influence how others exercise their rights, and the authority enforces rules such as building permits on behalf of those who cannot protect themselves. The two must be consistent; if public law 'takes' someone's property rights, compensation may be needed (p. 28).",
    "Which interests get which protection? Partly political: my interest in my neighbour's door colour is judged less important than hers, so it gets no protection at all (p. 28). Partly technical: damage to my foundations has a clear cause, a clear culprit and few people involved, so a private 'right of support' works. Air pollution affecting hundreds of people is diffuse, and they would struggle to co-operate in court, so clean air is protected by public measures like permit conditions and emission limits (p. 28). But this allocation can be revised. Since rights are created, not natural, they are a 'policy variable': 'property rights can be deliberately changed in order purposefully to influence land use' (pp. 28–29). He notes rights often carry high ideological content and are constrained by conventions like the ECHR, so they change slowly. An example: future generations' interest in scarce resources could be made a property right held by trustees (p. 29)."

   "h": "Planning and markets are always a mix",
   "p": [
    "Needham then introduces 'the market' in a broad sense (p. 29). A market exists when one person has something another wants, exchange is voluntary with free choice of partner, and such exchange happens regularly among many people. Since most land-use decisions are voluntary, most come about 'in the market', and Lindblom notes that it is almost impossible to imagine a process like land-use change without voluntary decisions.",
    "It follows that 'planning or the market?' is not a useful question; the only interesting question is the mix of the two (p. 29). Exercising a property right is itself acting in the market, because rights frame the exchange of interests in land. So deciding whether to protect an interest by private law (rights) or public law (regulation) is deciding on that mix, and shifting protection from public to private law by creating or changing rights amounts to creating markets in property rights (p. 29)."

   "h": "Creating markets in property rights: the literature and his approach",
   "p": [
    "This choice has been studied before (p. 30). Coase showed the reciprocal nature of harm: restraining A to protect B harms A, so the real question is 'Should A be allowed to harm B or should B be allowed to harm A?' Ellickson examined covenants, nuisance rules and fines as alternatives to zoning, finding private law works in some situations but not all. Webster and Lai asked whether public-domain resources could be managed better as 'club goods' through changed property rights. Needham stresses that these studies should not be confused with libertarian attacks on planning, which deny external effects or deny that public authorities can protect the public (pp. 30–31).",
    "His own evaluation method is not the economist's assumption that every policy aims at maximum efficiency. He follows Williamson's idea of 'remediability': compare different ways of reaching the same politically set goals by how effectively they reach them, and consider resource use and distributional effects (pp. 30–31). The chapter only reports briefly on four types of situation, drawn from his fuller work (Needham 2005a, 2006)."

   "h": "Evidence and cases: four situations",
   "p": [
    "Amenity in housing estates (pp. 31–32). Instead of plan plus permit plus monitoring, buyers can enter a covenant restricting harmful actions, with enforcement vested in a residents' association; the residents' interest becomes a property right. This is common in the US ('private community associations') and exists in England. It is not possible in the Netherlands, because Dutch property law cannot oblige owners to join such an association; changing that would create a market solution. Well-maintained industrial estates (p. 32). In England and the US an investor buys the estate, leases buildings to firms and maintains it to protect the value of his investment. In the Netherlands owners cannot be obliged to join a maintenance association, and leases are not tradable, so there is little investment interest; estates deteriorate and need public money. Making unexpired leases saleable would change this.",
    "Urban public spaces (pp. 32–33). Adjacent owners and users have an interest in parks and squares but no rights; privately owned but publicly accessible spaces, such as shopping malls or resident-owned spaces in small developments, can be created with planning conditions that guarantee access and maintenance. Coherent regional development (pp. 33–34). Here a market in rights is much harder: effects of one decision are dispersed over so many people, each slightly affected, that trading rights is unrealistic. Still, authorities sometimes use private law, as with US conservation agreements where a trust buys farmers' development rights so as not to use them, keeping land open (p. 34)."
