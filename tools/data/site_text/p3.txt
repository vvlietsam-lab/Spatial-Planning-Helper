     "Scott Campbell's 1996 JAPA article is one of the most cited texts in planning, mainly for one image: the 'planner's triangle'. His opening claim is blunt: 'Nothing inherent in the discipline steers planners either toward environmental protection or toward economic development -- or toward a third goal of planning: social equity.' Planners work inside the tension between these three aims. Sustainable development sits at the centre of the triangle, but that centre cannot be reached directly.",
     "Campbell wrote in a period when sustainable development had become planning's newest fascination. He takes it seriously but refuses to treat it as a way of wishing conflicts away. The conflicts between green, growth and justice, he says, are not superficial matters of personal preference, nor just misunderstandings between the languages of economists, ecologists and activists, nor a temporary problem. They 'go to the historic core of planning'. Sustainability can only become useful if it is redefined and placed inside a realistic understanding of political conflict.     "h": "Planners have no natural home on the triangle",
    "p": [
     "Campbell starts by puncturing the profession's self-image. Planners' recent environmental enthusiasm might suggest a natural green bias, but historically they have built cities by clearing forests and fouling rivers, even if park planners, greenbelt planners and environmental planners also defended nature. On the spectrum from Robert Moses to Earth First!, the planner has no natural home, and the midpoint has no special claim to fairness. Likewise, planners see themselves as defenders of the poor, but downtown redevelopment, freeways and enterprise zones hardly add up to equity planning (drawing on Harvey).",
     "So the planner must reconcile not two but at least three interests: grow the economy, distribute growth fairly, and not degrade the ecosystem. The popular 'jobs versus environment' framing (spotted owl versus timber jobs) hides the third dimension, because it lumps workers, corporations, communities and the national public together under 'economy'. In practice, professional and fiscal constraints mean most planners serve one goal (tax revenue, open space, housing for the poor) and neglect the other two. The gap between the call for integrated sustainable planning (the centre) and fragmented practice (the edges) is the core problem.",
     "Each corner implies a view of the city. The economic development planner sees a place of production and innovation competing with other cities; space is highways and market areas. The environmental planner sees a consumer of resources and producer of waste competing with nature; space is river basins and greenways. The equity planner sees conflict over distribution within the city; space is communities, segregation and access. Campbell says the triangle is not the true geometry of planning, just a useful simplification."

    "h": "Three conflicts on the three sides",
    "p": [
     "Each side of the triangle is a conflict. The property conflict (growth versus equity) arises from competing claims on property: management and labour, landlords and tenants, gentrifiers and long-time residents. Each side resists the other but also needs it. Following Foglesong, the 'property contradiction' is that capitalist democracies treat property as a private commodity but rely on government (zoning, public housing) to secure its social benefits, so the private sector both resists and needs social intervention.",
     "The resource conflict (growth versus environment) has the same structure: business resists regulation of its exploitation of nature, yet needs regulation to conserve resources for the future. Its boundary is symbolised by the 'city limits' between developed city and wilderness. Campbell draws a neat parallel: industry must pay a subsistence wage so labour can 'reproduce' itself, and must leave enough forest so it can reproduce too ('sustained yield'); in both cases how much is 'enough' is contested. He rejects the idea of one universal 'human versus nature' conflict behind all cases: the triangle is a template, and real conflicts need case studies.",
     "The development conflict (equity versus environment) is 'the most elusive'. How can the poor gain opportunity if environmental protection slows growth? Globally, rich nations ask poor nations to forgo rapid development. Locally, miners and loggers see environmentalists as elitists, and poor communities face a no-win choice between jobs and noxious land uses such as incinerators and landfills, which puts environmental racism at the heart of the development conflict. Norman Krumholz in Cleveland had to choose between regional rail (luring suburban drivers out of cars) and better local buses (helping the inner-city poor): pollution reduction versus equitable access."

    "p": [
     "The corners also depend on each other. One classic argument is that growth makes redistribution and environmental protection easier: 'trickle-down economics' and what Campbell calls 'trickle-down environmentalism', visible in Brundtland's call to revitalise global growth. He accepts part of it but adds that only fairly distributed growth lets the poor protect their environment: the development conflict can be resolved only if the property conflict is resolved too.      "The triangle also integrates two intellectual traditions: an essentialist environmental view ('man versus nature', emphasising the resource conflict) and a historical-materialist social view (capital versus labour, emphasising the property conflict). Together they reveal the social dimension of environmental conflict. Nature is socially constructed in the sense that groups have different conceptions and uses of it: the lumberjack is not less 'close to nature' than the weekend hiker. This does not mean nature is arbitrary. Campbell calls the choice between anthropocentric and ecocentric views a false one: 'We are all unavoidably anthropocentric; the question is which anthropomorphic values and priorities we will apply to the natural and the social world around us.'"

    "h": "Sustainable development: a useful but vague centre",
    "p": [
     "Campbell defines the centre of the triangle as sustainable development, the balance of the three goals. He observes that it has become hard to oppose: who would dare to speak against it? A pessimistic reading is that the concept has lost its teeth: if the World Bank and radical ecologists both endorse it, it asks for no specific commitment, and it seems to violate Popper's falsifiability. An optimistic reading is that it has become hegemonic, the parameter rather than a variable of debate, so the job is to work out details. Is it useful for planners? The answer is mixed: it may be too distant and holistic to operationalise, and simply adding 'sustainable' to existing plans is not sustainable planning. Yet as a long-term, unifying vision of a social-environmental system in balance, it can be refined into something useful.",
     "His main target is a historical romanticism. Many look for sustainability in pre-industrial or indigenous cultures, but Campbell argues the path to sustainability lies forward, not behind us: urbanisation, trade and population growth are irreversible. Pre-industrial communities had no choice but to be sustainable (overuse meant dying out); modern societies must choose it voluntarily. Romanticising the past carries anti-urban sentiment and projects social equality onto nature, which in itself is neither equal nor unequal. Social norms, not nature, must decide what is fair. Environmentalism stretches equity across generations and species, which creates new contradictions: slowing growth may help future generations but hurt poor countries now."

    "h": "Why the centre can only be reached indirectly",
    "p": [
     "Campbell compares sustainable development to the 1950s–60s faith in comprehensive planning for one 'public interest'. He predicts the same critiques: incrementalists will say a sustainable society cannot be reached in one leap; advocacy planners will say no common interest exists and bureaucrats will neglect the poor and nature. 'Sustainable Development Master Plans' risk becoming glib wish lists that gather dust. He also worries that steady-state, no-growth economics could keep the developing world and the poor in poverty: a sustainable economy will not automatically be just.",
     "His redefinition has three steps. First, avoid black-and-white thinking: society is a hybrid of sustainable and unsustainable practices and should move step by step. Second, broaden sustainability to the long-term ability of any system to reproduce itself; societies already sustain their economic and political systems but neglect the ecological one, so the aim is to sustain all three in balance. Third, distinguish specific (local, sectoral) from general (global) sustainability; global sustainability will come only through a long, incremental accumulation of local advances. So the vision of a sustainable city will emerge at the end of contested negotiations over land use, transport and housing, not as their premise: one cannot reach the centre 'in a single, holistic leap to a pre-ordained balance'."

    "h": "Procedural and substantive paths",
    "p": [
     "The planner's role is a dual strategy: manage and resolve conflict, and promote creative technical, architectural and institutional solutions. Procedurally, negotiation and conflict resolution can uncover compatible underlying interests and win-win outcomes, but they fail with all-or-nothing disputes (a dam is built or not), large power imbalances or powerful parties who refuse to take part. Translation between the languages of economics, ecology and social justice helps, but must run both ways so that the economic language does not dominate like a colonial lingua franca; and when interests truly clash, translation must give way. Political pluralism (referendums, elections) widens participation but handles complexity poorly; market mechanisms such as pollution credits address the resource conflict but often neglect equity.",
     "Substantively, land-use planning is arguably still the planner's most powerful tool: greenbelts, Howard's Garden City, clustered and denser development, and above all reconciling the territorial logics of human and natural habitats, since both need connected land and each fragments the other. Bioregionalism rescales economy and community to ecological regions, but risks a utopian 'spatial fix' and underdevelopment through self-reliance. Technology reduces resource use per unit but gains are often cancelled by rising demand (car fuel efficiency versus total gasoline use) and do not ensure fairness. The best solutions combine several: a negotiated clustered housing design plus a per-unit fee for open space."
