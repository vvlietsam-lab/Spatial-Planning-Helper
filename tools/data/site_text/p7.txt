     "Yansui Liu and Yang Zhou, geographers at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, write about one of the largest planning reforms of recent years: China's move to a single national territory spatial planning (NTSP) system. They define spatial planning as 'the long-term planning and overall arrangement of land resources and space layout under the jurisdiction of a country or region government, aiming to achieve effective control and scientific management of territorial space and promote the balance between development and protection' (p. 1). 'Territorial space' here means all land and sea under national jurisdiction.",
     "The article does three things (p. 2): it reviews how Chinese spatial planning evolved and which problems it created; it analyses the measures China has taken (institutional reform, a unified planning system, new legislation) and their challenges; and it proposes countermeasures. The authors note that international understanding of China's planning is limited because its land management system is so different, and present their paper as a contribution to closing that gap. Keep in mind that it is written from inside the Chinese policy world and is largely supportive of the reform."

    "h": "Spatial planning in Europe and China: the background",
    "p": [
     "The introduction first sketches the European story as a reference point (p. 1). Strategic spatial planning dominated European urban and regional planning in the 1960s and 1970s, was dismissed by neoconservatism and doubted by postmodernism in the 1980s, and was revived in the 1990s after disordered urban development, land fragmentation and environmental concerns. The term 'spatial planning' first appeared in the Council of Europe's 1983 charter, and the European Spatial Development Perspective (ESDP, 1999) made its purpose explicit: economic and social cohesion, sustainable development and balanced competitiveness. Countries such as Britain, Germany, France and the Netherlands built mature spatial planning and legal systems early.",
     "China's path was different (p. 2). After 1949 it borrowed the Soviet model of five-year national economic plans, with urban planning as the spatial continuation of the economic plan. From the 1990s, regional, land-use, environmental, transport and main functional zoning plans were added. Under the market economy, planning shifted to multiple targets, including urban expansion, real estate, 'vanity projects' and place promotion, and plan-making became an arena where central and local governments interfered to different degrees. Because there was no overarching plan, contents were repeated, spaces overlapped and land-use boundaries conflicted, leading to wasted land, social conflict, overheated land development and unbalanced regional development. In 2013 the state proposed building an NTSP system as part of 'ecological civilisation'."

    "h": "How China's spatial planning evolved",
    "p": [
     "Section 2 places the NTSP within national goals: ecological civilisation, high-quality development, 'Beautiful China', rural revitalisation and 'harmonious coexistence of human and nature' (p. 2). The history runs as follows. After reform and opening-up in 1978, China practised regional planning focused on productivity and urban layout. Resource surveys of land, water and climate followed; in 1987 rules for land planning were issued, but a 1990 draft General Plan of Land and Resources was never approved (p. 2).",
     "In the early 2000s pilots of territory planning ran in Tianjin, Xinjiang, Liaoning and Guangdong. The authors describe four shifts in this period: from 'leading' to 'guiding' planning, from resource use to equal attention to development and protection, from pure economic growth to coordinating economy, population, resources and environment, and from an industry focus to regional spatial layout (pp. 2–3). In 2010 the National Main Function Zoning divided the country into optimised, key, restricted and prohibited development zones, but it was hard to implement because its protection theme was not prominent. From 2014, 28 counties and cities piloted 'multi-planning integration' (p. 3). The authors summarise three stages: first land-use planning aimed at agriculture; then urban and regional planning serving urbanisation and industrialisation; and now the NTSP, aimed at high-quality development, ecological protection and sustainability (p. 3)."

    "h": "Three core problems of the old system",
    "p": [
     "Section 2.2 names three problems caused by the absence of a superior planning law (pp. 3–4). First, there were too many plan types and systems, each under its own department with its own levels. The authors' Table 1 compares the four main ones: main functional zone planning, urban-rural planning, land-use planning and eco-environmental protection planning. They conflicted in scale, boundaries, land-use rights, control rules and targets. They even classified land differently (a three-level system in urban-rural planning, a two-level one in land-use planning), so the same plot of farmland, forest or grassland could carry multiple attributes and uses (p. 3).",
     "Second, poor coordination produced what the authors call planning 'dislocation, offside and vacancy' (p. 4): plans in the wrong place, overstepping each other, or missing. Planning was spread over more than ten national departments, including the Ministry of Land and Resources, the Ministry of Housing and Urban-Rural Development, the National Development and Reform Commission and the environment ministry. Plan periods differed (10, 20, 15 and 5 years for the four plan types), and all ultimately rested on the five-year socioeconomic plan, which added instability (p. 4).",
     "Third, legalisation and standardisation lagged. China had more than eighty kinds of spatial plans, but the legal framework was scattered, there was no law at all for main functional zone planning, rights and responsibilities were unclear, and most legislation was administrative in character with inadequate provisions for public participation. The content, preparation, approval, implementation and modification of plans, and the responsibilities of those making them, all lacked clear legal provisions (p. 4)."

    "h": "The reform: one ministry, one system, one map",
    "p": [
     "Section 3 explains the response. The authors state that since 1949 there had been no basic or superior plan for territorial space, which they see as a root of disordered urban expansion, loss of farmland, inefficient resource use, ecological degradation and social conflict (p. 4). The first step was institutional: the 2018 State Council reform abolished the Ministry of Land and Resources and created the Ministry of Natural Resources (MNR), which took over land-use planning, urban-rural planning (from the housing ministry), main functional zone planning (from the NDRC) and eco-environmental planning. This gave the new system an organisational home (p. 4).",
     "The second step was a unified planning system. The May 2019 'Opinions' set out three steps (p. 4): by 2020, establish the NTSP system, complete overall plans for all levels from city and county up, and form a preliminary 'One Map' of territorial development and protection; by 2025, complete the laws, standards and monitoring and early-warning mechanisms; by 2035, modernise territorial governance and achieve efficient production space, liveable living space and 'beautiful' ecological space. The system has general, special and detailed plans; general and special plans exist at five levels (national, provincial, municipal, county, township), and detailed plans at two (control and construction) (p. 4). Plans must be based on an assessment of resource and environmental carrying capacity and the suitability of land for development (p. 5).",
     "The heart of the system is three control lines (p. 5). The ecological protection red line marks areas with key ecological functions or high ecological sensitivity (for example water conservation, biodiversity, coastal protection, nature reserves) that must be strictly protected. Permanent basic farmland is protected permanently for food security. The urban-town development boundary limits where cities may grow, to prevent disordered sprawl. The authors expect the reform to end the problems of too many plans, conflicts, long approvals and constantly changing local plans, to break departmental interests, and to reverse environmental decline 'from the source' (pp. 5–6)."

    "h": "The legal step: a spatial planning law",
    "p": [
     "The third measure is legislation (p. 6). Because there was no superior law, plans could not be implemented 'in accordance with the law'. In March 2020 the government was consulting on an NTSP law, which the authors call a great innovation. The law would create a multi-level system from the national level down to village plans; regulate how plans are drawn up, approved, revised and supervised; state that plans may not be changed without legal procedures (to end frequent revisions); and emphasise public participation, requiring draft plans to be published and expert and public opinion to be sought through demonstration meetings or hearings. A supervisory committee would review the process and punish violations. For the authors, this law fills a long-standing lack of 'macro top-level design', resolves conflicts between existing laws, clarifies who is responsible for making and for implementing plans, and supports 'ruling the country by law' and ecological civilisation (p. 6)."

    "p": [
     "The discussion is more reflective (pp. 6–7). Citing Van Assche and Djanibekov, the authors note that planning systems do not escape path dependence. They identify the root of China's planning problems plainly: it 'lies in the game of interests between the central and local governments, between different departments and within the country, and multi-management, as well as the insufficient understanding of the rules of spatial layout by planners and decision makers' (p. 6). Land was split between urban and non-urban planning zones; planners were constrained by land-use rights; and plan-making was out of touch with research such as land-change science.",
     "Looking ahead, the NTSP must prioritise homeland, ecological and food security. National and provincial plans should handle strategic issues (food, energy, economic and ecological security), while municipal and county plans deal with layout, intensity and environmental quality (p. 6). Drawing the control lines is hard: ecological red lines must keep ecosystems connected to avoid 'ecological islands', and urban boundaries must account for shrinking places as well as growth, since between 2000 and 2010 more than 10,000 towns and street offices lost population (p. 7).",
     "The NTSP must also combine top-down arrangement with bottom-up needs, especially in rural areas. Village numbers fell from 3.77 million in 1990 to 2.61 million in 2016, over 100 a day, and with urbanisation expected to reach 70–75% by 2035, hundreds of thousands more may disappear; village plans must anticipate this to avoid wasted infrastructure (p. 7). Compared with the past, the NTSP will use big data and the third national land survey, control land and sea in three dimensions, integrate urban and rural areas, and shift from prioritising economic growth to equal attention for high-quality development and environmental protection, efficiency and fairness, with public involvement raising people's 'sense of gain and happiness' (p. 7)."
