   "p": [
    "Gurran and Ruming start from the contradictory position of planning under neoliberal reform (p. 1). Planning systems have proved remarkably resilient to deregulation and remain a key form of state intervention, and in some countries (the UK, the Netherlands, many US cities) planning tools even help secure affordable homes within market developments. Yet over the past decade, amid anxiety about housing production and affordability, the legitimacy of planning has been questioned: critics point to slow and complex processes, restrictive controls, and residents using planning rules to block development (pp. 1–2).",
    "Planning is thus expected to support economic growth by facilitating big development, while also protecting the property interests of established suburbs; wider public goals such as environmental protection or better housing for the poor get squeezed between these interests (p. 2). The article examines these tensions in Australia, one of the world's most expensive housing markets, where prices rose fast after mortgage deregulation and home ownership fell from about 71% in the mid 1980s to 68% in 2012 (p. 2).",
    "The authors' question is how particular interests shape urban policy debate and reform. Their case is the planning system review in New South Wales (NSW), Australia's most populous state, between 2011 and 2014 (pp. 2–3). They compare written submissions from industry, residents and professionals with the government's responses and find that the government mostly echoed industry narratives. In that setting, housing development is framed as the engine of economic growth and the planning system as the constraint holding it back, while community worries about affordability and new development are reframed to fit the growth agenda (p. 1)."

   "h": "Discourse as power: the method's theory",
   "p": [
    "The approach is discourse analysis, which the authors describe as broadly Foucauldian (p. 3). Language is not just a medium for communication but central in 'producing or constructing reality', including what we know and the meanings we give to the world (quoting Hastings). Following Jacobs, politics is seen as an arena where groups try to establish a particular narrative of events to pursue their objectives.",
    "The key concept is the 'regime of truth' (Foucault). The power of a group lies in its ability to promote and sustain its version of reality as the 'objective truth' through speech, text and images (p. 3). Once established, such discourses become fairly static representations that define which formulations of problems and solutions are acceptable. Discourse is therefore not the product of neutral, rational deliberation but a messy site of power struggles, where dominant regimes of truth can also be challenged. The authors want to show how one set of narratives about planning's problems, objectives and solutions became a regime of truth reflected in the government's draft legislation (p. 3)."

   "h": "Neoliberalism, housing and planning",
   "p": [
    "The authors place their case in a broader literature (pp. 3–4). Neoliberalism, emerging in the 1970s and 1980s, opposes state intervention in markets and challenges the legitimacy of planning regulation of private developers. Its effects on housing vary by context, but typically include more support for home ownership and private investment, rent subsidies instead of public housing, and quasi-market management of social housing. In the UK, Thatcherism shifted social housing to tenants and housing associations, but councils could still use 'section 106' agreements to secure land for social housing; in the US, inclusionary zoning emerged alongside tax credits.",
    "These approaches have limits (p. 4). Home ownership policies for low-income groups were questioned after the global financial crisis; rent subsidies expose vulnerable groups to insecurity; and inclusionary schemes depend on buoyant markets. Still, planning is an important mechanism for making sure affordable housing is well located near jobs and services. In Australia, however, attempts to require affordable housing through planning have been resisted by industry and governments fearing higher prices and less development, and by resident groups; the dominant rhetoric stresses the negative effects of planning on the housing market (p. 4)."

   "h": "The Australian context",
   "p": [
    "Both major parties have applied neoliberal reforms since the 1980s (p. 4). Public housing investment was replaced by subsidies for private renters: social housing approvals in NSW fell from around 15% of dwellings in 1983 to less than two per cent in 2014 (Figure 1). Unusually, most postwar public dwellings were later sold off, so by 1981 only about 120,000 of 840,000 remained public rentals (p. 5). Public infrastructure investment also declined, replaced by user-pays charges and public-private partnerships.",
    "Planning is a state and territory responsibility, with local councils handling detailed land allocation and assessment (p. 5). By the 1990s planning had narrowed to a local battle between established homeowners and developers, sharpened by 'urban consolidation' (Australia's term for growth management and densification). Industry criticized consolidation for limiting greenfield land, while homeowners feared density. From the 2000s, states tried to cut 'red' and 'green' tape, and COAG, the council of federal, state and local governments, put planning deregulation on its national competition agenda (pp. 5–6).",
    "Housing affordability became the main rationale for these reforms (p. 6). Yet the authors point to a telling finding by the former National Housing Supply Council: Australia's estimated shortage of about 85,000 dwellings in 2008 would have been a modest surplus if social housing production had kept pace with its 1996 level, which would have added about 90,000 dwellings. By 2011/2012 only 3.9% of households lived in social housing. In short, the supply gap could be read as a retreat of public provision rather than a failure of planning, but the national agenda still framed it as a planning problem (pp. 6–7)."
