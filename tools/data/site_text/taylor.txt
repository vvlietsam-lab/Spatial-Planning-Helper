   "h": "What the article asks and why",
   "p": [
    "Melbourne's Urban Growth Boundary (UGB), a legal line separating land where urban development is allowed from land kept rural, was legislated in 2002 and expanded substantially in 2010 (p. 259). After the expansion the press reported 'instant millionaire' landowners, and one study found that per-hectare land prices inside the boundary rose by 89 percent relative to comparable land outside (p. 259). Yet during one window of time, just before the expansion, many of the same landowners argued the opposite: that being brought inside the boundary would not raise the value of their land at all. Taylor's article is about that reversal and about what it did to policy.",
    "The trigger was a new instrument that came with the expansion: the Growth Areas Infrastructure Contribution (GAIC). Taylor calls it in effect a 'hypothecated' tax (a tax whose revenue is earmarked for one purpose, here infrastructure in new suburbs) on betterment, the rise in land value caused by a public decision such as rezoning (pp. 259–260). She critically interprets 264 public submissions on the UGB expansion and the GAIC, and argues that the later changes to the GAIC, including the switch to a 'purchaser pays' model, are best understood as a response to rent-seeking pressure from existing landowners acting as 'insiders' (p. 260).",
    "Rent-seeking here means using political influence to secure or protect income from land that is not earned by productive effort. Taylor borrows two routes from Clingermayer: 'heresthetics', the intentional manipulation of an issue, and 'happenstance', where altruistic goals unintentionally line up with rent-seeking ones (p. 260). She also uses Evans' 'insider-outsider' problem: planning participation tends to be biased towards existing property owners, while future residents have no voice. Her position is careful: she does not claim all landowners were lying, but tests the assumption that at least part of their claims was driven by perceived economic self-interest, including a wish to avoid the GAIC (p. 260)."

   "h": "Melbourne's boundary and the promise of windfall gains",
   "p": [
    "The 2008 planning update 'Melbourne @ 5 Million' proposed to consider about 50,000 hectares of 'investigation areas' for inclusion in the UGB, justified by population projections and a stated need to keep land supply 'adequate and competitive' (p. 261). The same update introduced the GAIC, which Taylor notes was, 'probably tellingly', not named a betterment tax (p. 261). The state government presented it as only a small portion of the significant increase in land values that rezoning creates, to be used to offset the heavy costs of infrastructure in growth areas.",
    "The charge rested on concrete estimates. The Growth Areas Authority cited research that rural land around Melbourne was typically worth $15,000 to $35,000 per hectare, but that rural land brought inside the UGB and rezoned rose to an average of $365,000 per hectare, within a range of $225,000 to $450,000 (p. 261). Planners and developers interviewed in another study agreed that land inside the UGB had become much more expensive, though they blamed each other. One developer put it bluntly: “People that are inside the UGB, the farmers and the owners, believe their land is liquid gold” (p. 261).",
    "The GAIC design mattered for what followed. It was a flat, one-off charge of $95,000 per hectare on land over 0.41 hectares (one acre) brought inside the UGB, payable at the sale or subdivision of the land, whichever came first (p. 261). This meant it could hit ordinary vendors who were simply selling, not only developers at the point of development. Development gains in Victoria had never been directly taxed; the 1971 metropolitan plan had discussed and rejected a betterment tax (p. 262). A landowner group, Taxed Out!, campaigned against the proposal throughout 2009 and early 2010."

   "h": "The economics: why a boundary creates betterment",
   "p": [
    "Taylor then sets out the economic logic. A UGB is part of an urban containment strategy, which typically aims at a more compact city, higher densities near public transport and the preservation of rural landscapes (p. 262). Whatever their goals, they affect land and housing markets. Farmland near cities tends to gain value as demand for building land grows, and planning rules sharpen the gap between agricultural and urban land. Citing Jun, she notes that although the evidence is not overwhelming, many studies find that containment raises land prices by limiting the supply of developable land (p. 262).",
    "Price differences at the boundary can come from the supply side (less land allowed) or from the demand side (the certainty and amenity a boundary creates). For economists this distinction matters because it decides whether a boundary is efficient; for fringe landowners, Taylor remarks, what counts is simply that land outside is worth less than land inside (p. 262). Figure 1 shows the mechanism: without a boundary, urban and rural land prices meet at a point c. A binding boundary drawn before c splits the price at the line into a higher urban price (d) and a lower rural price (e). The gap f between them is the betterment, which is unlocked for any parcel the boundary is moved to include (p. 263).",
    "Betterment goes by many names: development gain, windfall, planning gain (p. 263). The political case for taxing it is that the owner has made an unearned gain that arises largely from community or government action. Impact fees and inclusionary zoning are related forms of land value taxation (p. 263). The GAIC rested on exactly this assumption: that inclusion within the UGB brings a substantial rise in land value."

   "h": "Two opposing interests landowners can have",
   "p": [
    "Planning is an intervention in property rights, which it limits, allocates and reallocates, and these allocations have economic value that invites 'capture' (p. 264). Taylor highlights the idea of 'dual predictability': containment makes it more certain which land can and cannot be developed, while also making clear that land inside the boundary is exhaustible (p. 264). This gives owners outside the line a strong incentive to lobby to be included and harvest the expected betterment. She calls this premium-seeking behaviour.",
    "But some landowners want the opposite. Owners of lifestyle properties in low-density communities near cities often value the aesthetic or symbolic quality of rural land, and the scarcity and value stability that planning controls give them (p. 264). Here Taylor uses Fischel's term 'homevoters': owner-occupiers whose political choices are driven by protecting their property values. Their interests mix sometimes noble, altruistic intentions with inevitable property market consequences. So theory predicts two, sometimes opposite, kinds of rent-seeking: avoiding controls that limit future development value, and preserving restrictions on growth to protect a home and a landscape (p. 264).",
    "Either way, if the boundary moves and urban values are unlocked, a tax on the betterment is unwelcome. How costly it really is depends on who ends up bearing it, its 'incidence', which is disputed: some research suggests landowners receive slightly lower prices but homebuyers ultimately bear most of the cost (p. 264). This is why Taylor focuses on what landowners claimed about incidence: who they said would really pay."

   "h": "Evidence and cases: what the submissions show",
   "p": [
    "The material is the public inquiry into 'Delivering Melbourne's Newest Sustainable Communities' (2010), which received 1,411 individual and 503 organisational submissions (p. 265). Taylor took the first 20 percent received from each group; because code numbers were assigned randomly, this is effectively a random sample. From 382 coded submissions she removed those dealing only with other proposals and those that were confidential, duplicates or empty, leaving 264 (218 individuals, 46 organisations). As a rent-seeking perspective would predict, 186 of them (70 percent) came from fringe landowners directly affected, including 26 commercial farmers, 28 'lifestyle' residents and 49 owners with plans to develop (p. 265).",
    "On the expansion 'in principle', most comments were objections (83 of 111) (p. 265). Objections from non-landowners, often inner-Melbourne residents and environmental groups, referred to urban consolidation in general and to high-value areas such as the Merri Creek and western grasslands. Landowners who objected instead talked about local amenity (the rural feel, birdlife, lack of noise) and their rural lifestyle, often in terms that fit Hubbard's 'self/other' contrast between rural and urban (p. 266). Taylor stresses that such owners may argue in good faith, but their altruistic arguments unavoidably align with their property interests.",
    "Views on owners' own land sorted neatly by interest (Table 2, p. 267). Thirty-three owners inside the new UGB were happy to be included, 52 outside wanted in, and 11 inside wanted to stay out. Those happy to be in were mainly broad-hectare farmers or owners of vacant land, suggesting plans to sell and move. Those wanting out were mostly lifestyle owners and businesses such as a winery, valuing land use over development. Twenty-one owners in the west, whose land was to be protected grassland, called the rules unfair; Taylor reads this as premium-seeking objection to planning restrictions, in stark contrast to non-landowners who supported grassland protection (p. 268)."

   "h": "Discrediting betterment: the campaign against the GAIC",
   "p": [
    "The GAIC itself was almost universally unpopular with the people who would pay it: 83 sampled submissions objected, 76 of them from impacted individual landowners, and only four supported it (Table 3, p. 268). Crucially, objectors treated the charge as a tax on their existing land value, not on a future windfall. About a third claimed it would hurt their existing values and home equity, and about a third called it unfair, undemocratic or immoral, often in emotional terms about years of hard work (p. 269).",
    "Taylor identifies three ways in which submissions doubted and ultimately discredited the windfall premise (pp. 269–270). First, some owners simply did not understand, or ignored, the betterment logic, calculating the charge as a share of their current property value (one concluded he would lose more than half his home). Second, some openly disbelieved that inclusion would raise values at all, or mocked the idea, asking who would guarantee the increase. Third, some accepted that values might rise elsewhere but not on their own land for some years. This framing was central to the Taxed Out! campaign (p. 270).",
    "She connects these reactions to the power of perceived property rights, which can gain political traction even when they contradict the legal basis of property (p. 270), and to a strong political aversion to any tax on owner-occupation. The most telling pattern comes from owners with development plans (Table 4, p. 271): only 4 of these 49 (8 percent) objected to the GAIC, against 72 of the 137 (53 percent) who mentioned no plans. Owners who knew what development would earn them saw the charge as affordable. Taylor concludes that opposition was driven by landowners, not developers, who tend to accept charges that are transparent and can be passed on to buyers (p. 271)."

    "The most common alternative proposal (32 submissions, 42 percent of landowner objectors) was that developers, not landowners, should pay, since they could pass the charge on to the eventual home purchasers (p. 271). In June 2011 a new minister made the charge fully deferred until development (pp. 272–273). The minister said this protected hobby farmers and lifestyle owners: a 'homevoter' effect (p. 273)."

    "This section is my synthesis, not Taylor's claim. The article applies rational choice theory, specifically public choice ideas about rent-seeking and 'capture', to planning politics. Actors are treated as pursuing economic self-interest within a set of rules, and the interesting question is how they use participation and political pressure to shift those rules in their favour. In the terms of the neo-institutionalism lecture, it sits close to rational choice institutionalism: institutions (the UGB, the GAIC) create value and incentives, and organised interests then fight to change them.",
    "On the course dimensions: agency is strong but structured, since landowners act strategically within a boundary regime that creates the rent in the first place. The outcome is reproduction rather than transformation: existing owners protect their advantage and costs shift to future buyers. Change is deliberate lobbying, but the final policy is no one's design. Knowledge is situated in one Melbourne episode, though Taylor draws a general implication about insiders and outsiders. Her normative commitment is visible in the concern for affordability and for capturing unearned gains for public infrastructure.",
    "Useful contrasts: Sorensen's Toronto study shows land value capture as a long, contested institutional pathway (historical institutionalism), while Taylor zooms in on one moment of interest-group pressure. Moroni would read the protection of property rights more positively, as a valuable evolved institution. A Marxist reading, such as Foglesong's property contradiction, would link the fight over betterment to the tension between the social production of land value and its private ownership.
