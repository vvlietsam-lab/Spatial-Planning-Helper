Pruijt distinguishes two forms of integration. Institutionalization: a movement channelled into a stable pattern based on formalized rules; terminal (convention replaces disruption) or flexible (conventional tactics complement disruptive ones). Cooptation (after Selznick): the co-opting organization embraces some movement ideas while redefining problems so that solving them does not threaten its stability. Post-Fordist theory predicts that a market-oriented regime, cutting welfare, pushes the 'privatization of poverty' and co-opts movement groups as service providers, while city competition increases repression.",

   "h": "Repression, institutionalization and cooptation",
   "p": [
    "Following Castells, Pruijt notes that states respond to radical urban movements with both repression and integration (p. 2). Repression shows in laws closing legal loopholes for squatters, but in the UK, the Netherlands and Germany these measures never fully stamped squatting out. Often the two were combined: Berlin authorities eliminated squatting between 1981 and 1984 through a mix of repression and legalization, and Platt observes that in the UK organized squatting declined more because of concessions than because of repression (p. 3). Integration comes in two forms, which he carefully separates.",
    "The first is institutionalization: a movement is channeled into a stable pattern based on formal rules and laws, with defined expectations and sanctions (pp. 2–3). For squatters it usually takes the form of legalization, as with the 'amnesty' for some 5,000 London squatters in 1977. Authorities may prefer legalization because squatting enjoys some legitimacy and because eviction can be too costly; squatters are drawn to it because squatting is also an economic activity (meeting housing needs), and repression pushes them to negotiate (p. 3). Castells held that institutionalization, with its identity loss, is inevitable, and protest-wave theory places it near the end of a cycle (p. 4). But other authors show that movements can be partly integrated while a radical wing keeps disrupting. Pruijt therefore distinguishes two types: 'Terminal institutionalization implies that, in the repertoire of action, convention replaces disruption; in flexible institutionalization, conventional tactics complement disruptive ones' (p. 4).",
    "The second form is cooptation. Building on Selznick, Pruijt defines it as a process in which the co-opting organization 'embraces certain ideas from the movement, while redefining problems in such a way that solving them does not threaten its own stability' (p. 4). The co-opting body gives activists a role within its own framework. The UK's 'licensed squatting' is the classic example: squatters' groups became offices managing short-life public housing and had to stop organizing squatting, which split the movement (pp. 4–5). Pruijt takes a neutral stance: the costs and benefits of cooptation must be judged case by case."

   "h": "Post-Fordism and the privatization of poverty",
   "p": [
    "Why would the regime matter? Pruijt draws on post-Fordist theory (p. 5). As welfare costs grow, governments cut spending, criticize bureaucratic redistribution as inefficient and strengthen the ethos of competition. Yet they still aim to reduce poverty, so they face conflicting demands. The logical response is to support self-help: the 'privatization of poverty'. Neighborhood groups are co-opted to plug holes left by the retreating state; in the US they took on management roles, became financially dependent and bureaucratized, and sometimes came into conflict with their own tenants (pp. 5–6).",
    "So the hypothesis is that a market-oriented regime creates stronger pull factors toward cooptation (the state needs service providers) and also stronger push factors in the form of repression, because cities competing for visitors and business want a clean, safe image (p. 6). Pruijt summarizes three ways integration can complement repression: flexible institutionalization, which keeps squatting effective; terminal institutionalization, which ends the movement; and cooptation, in which oppositional groups become service providers, more likely under a market-oriented regime (p. 6)."

    "The Amsterdam municipality bought about two hundred squatted buildings, legalizing them, and handed most to housing associations that signed leases with individual squatters (p. 7). Squatters took part in construction planning and city planning, for example in the eastern docklands, and politicians mediated in many conflicts. Outside legalization, too, contacts were dense: the city repaired some squats slated for demolition to keep them habitable, and subsidized neighborhood centers and church groups represented squatters' views.     "Yet, contrary to much of the movement literature, there was no loss of willingness to disrupt (p. 8). Squatters kept defending buildings the city would not buy, raised bridges to defend warehouses as late as 2000, rioted at the 1980 coronation, and joined protests against the new town hall and the Olympic bid. Disruptive and conventional tactics existed side by side, and legalization in the early 1980s was not an end-of-cycle event, since squatting continued two decades later. So Amsterdam shows that flexible institutionalization is possible. And contrary to post-Fordist expectations, there was hardly any cooptation: squatters' groups did not become providers of services the state neglected, and the third-sector housing associations did not originate in the movement (p. 8)."

    "In New York the pattern fits the theory (pp. 8–11). A 1970 squatting wave died out quickly: the New York Times reported 13 squatting articles in 1970, three in 1971 and 1972, then none until 1980. Repression played a role, but so did a shift from confrontation to cooperation. Organizers and supporters such as UHAB moved into city programmes like Tenant Interim Lease (TIL), in which tenants managed tax-foreclosed buildings with the prospect of cooperative ownership.     "Official 'homesteading' programmes tried to 'tame' squatting: groups renovated abandoned city buildings with 'sweat equity' in return for eventual ownership (p. 9). Renovation took six to ten years, sometimes thirteen, and homesteaders gradually came to see themselves as homeowners opposed to squatters, splitting the anti-displacement movement (p. 10). Organizations that had used squatting as a tactic transformed too: ACORN received 58 buildings only after promising to abandon squatting, and Banana Kelly evolved from squatting to renovation to becoming an award-winning service provider before collapsing in scandal and poor maintenance (pp. 10–11). Pruijt concludes that squatting was marginalized as organizers turned to the city and organizations became service providers, consistent with post-Fordist theory, but adds that this is not inevitable (p. 11)."

   "p": [
    "The puzzle is that after 1983 a genuine squatters' movement emerged on the Lower East Side that was neither co-opted nor repressed out of existence; only in 2002, when no squattable buildings were left, were eleven squats legalized through UHAB mediation, which Pruijt classifies as terminal institutionalization (p. 11). City officials consistently refused to negotiate with squatters. The explanation cannot be that the city lost interest in cooptation, because in 1992 it offered a Bronx group legalization of three buildings if all other squatters left and it became a service provider (pp. 11–12). The explanation must lie in the movement itself.",
    "Pruijt therefore distinguishes a housing movement, for which squatting is one possible means to house poor people, from a squatters' movement proper, in which 'squatting itself is means and primary goal at the same time' (p. 12). A squatters' movement is a bottom-up, network-structured community with informal leadership, a do-it-yourself ethic, an ideology of self-determination, and participants often rich in social and cultural capital (p. 12). Motives such as autonomy or counterculture are not enough to explain the difference (pp. 12–13). The crucial point is about goals: cooptation works when officials can claim to share the movement's ultimate goal (housing the poor) and offer support if activists switch to state-approved means. When squatting is itself the goal, officials cannot credibly claim to share it (p. 13). The New York movement also had a partly external origin: European squatters brought ideas and practices, showing that a pre-existing oppositional identity can partly override the local opportunity structure (pp. 13–14)."
