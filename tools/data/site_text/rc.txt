   "h": "Rational-comprehensive planning is not rational choice theory",
   "p": [
    "The first key slide separates two things that both carry the word 'rational'. Rational-comprehensive planning (the previous lecture, Rydin's governmental model) is an ideal standard for planning decisions: planning addresses market failures and optimises welfare, decision-making is objective and value-free, and it relies on expert knowledge and evidence. Rational choice theory is something else: an analytical framework for understanding how planning works. It assumes rational decision-making by all actors involved in planning, not just the planner. It looks not only at market failure but also at state failure, and it is associated with emulating the rationality of the market within state processes.",
    "What is rationality here? Following Rydin (2020), it is decision-making in which advantages and disadvantages, costs and benefits are weighed against each other; actors are driven by their interests. Because planning is a public activity that tries to control private actions in urban development, it has to deal with rational choice in all sectors. A striking consequence concerns the public interest. In rational choice there is no objective, universal goal; the public interest is simply the aggregate result of the private interests of different actors. The slide adds: it is an agency-focused theory."

   "h": "Planning and property rights: the dual role of the state",
   "p": [
    "The lecture then brings in property rights, drawing on Needham (2012). Different actors have different interests in land. The state plays a dual role: it creates and protects private property rights, which serve owners, and it makes public policies whose target groups are often those same owners, while the end-beneficiaries may be non-owners. This limits the power of land-use planning. Owners effectively have a 'veto right': if they do not want to develop, a plan alone will not make development happen. The key quote from Needham is: 'Land-use planning will only succeed if it can influence private decisions' (Needham 2012, p. 24).",
    "Planning can also reduce value, which the lecture calls regulatory takings: excluding land from zoning, conservation or heritage status, downzoning, or infrastructure decisions. Such 'planning damage' is often compensated. This sets up the central tension of the lecture: planning changes the value of land in both directions, and owners respond strategically to protect or capture that value. Two cases then show how these ideas work in practice: the debate between densification and urban expansion (rent-seeking) and Airbnb (Coase)."

   "h": "Rent-seeking and landowner strategies",
   "p": [
    "Landowners act rationally to capture rent, defined with Ricardo as the income from owning land or natural resources over and above the cost of production: the unearned value of land. This creates a big asymmetry that is much debated in planning. When planning raises land values through rezoning or infrastructure, landowners often keep the gain (betterment). When planning lowers values, they often claim compensation. Value capture mechanisms try to correct this. Taylor (2016) analyses the rational behaviour of two kinds of existing owners: those who want to preserve restrictions on growth, and those who try to avoid controls limiting future development. Both are rational from a rent-seeking perspective.",
    "The local case is Rijnenburg in Utrecht. Before 2008 developers negotiated with the municipality about housing; the financial crisis made their business case unviable, and an alternative vision of Rijnenburg as an energy landscape emerged. Buitelaar et al. (2025) identify four landowner strategies: framing (the area as 'low-hanging fruit' for housing), collaborating in a consortium of developers, lobbying, and negotiating. Bouwmeester then presents a hypothesis from her own research on why densification works in Switzerland, unlike the UK and the Netherlands where limits on expansion were reversed."

   "h": "A rational choice hypothesis: Swiss densification",
   "p": [
    "Swiss property law, specifically the Peasant's Land Act (BGBB), has two relevant rules. It prohibits transfers of agricultural land at more than a 5 to 15 percent increase over an adequate agricultural price (Art. 66), and it bars non-farmers from buying agricultural land (Art. 61). The hypothesised effect follows rational choice logic. There is no, or only a limited, speculative lobby of outside developers pushing for expansion. Insider landowners, meanwhile, profit from urban scarcity rents when growth is directed inward. So their rational strategy is to support, or at least accept, densification rather than fight it.",
    "The point for you is methodological, and the slide says it directly as a thesis tip: do not add theories as an afterthought, but use them to formulate a hypothesis about a specific relationship you could study. Rational choice is useful precisely because it produces testable expectations from assumptions about incentives and institutions. Here the institution (a property law limiting farmland prices and buyers) shapes who has an interest in expansion, and the hypothesis predicts how those actors will behave towards densification policy."

   "h": "Airbnb: incentives, externalities and collective action",
   "p": [
    "The Airbnb case is based on Gurran et al. (2018), who note that platforms can bring income to hosts and neighbourhoods, but tourist lodging in residential areas can harm neighbours, create safety risks and put 'pressure on local housing supply'. The lecture maps each actor's incentives. Professional hosts gain a new, more profitable income stream than permanent tenants. Guests get cheaper, more flexible, 'authentic' stays. Neighbours bear noise, disruption, weaker community ties, effects on their property values and pressure on housing. Planners face tourism growth, housing pressure and lost tax revenue. The costs borne by neighbours are externalities: effects on others that do not appear in the host's price.",
    "Why does regulation lag behind, as seen in New York, Barcelona, Amsterdam and Athens? The lecture uses Olson's insight: small, concentrated interests such as hosts and investors organise effectively into a strong lobby, whereas large groups such as neighbours struggle to mobilise because of free-riding. The result is a collective action problem: regulation lags despite collective harm. This links directly to the Olson reading (Hindmoor ch. 5)."

   "h": "Regulation and its problems",
   "p": [
    "Students then discussed which instruments a planner could use. Standard solutions include land-use controls banning short-term rental in residential zones, tourist taxes and fees, time limits such as 60 nights a year, and complaint systems. Gurran et al. put the general idea as: societies manage externalities from private development 'through regulation or taxation' (2018, p. 4). The lecture's question is therefore: what solution would a rational choice scholar propose instead?"

   "h": "Coase's theorem and Coasean solutions",
   "p": [
    "Coase (1960) starts from externalities: one actor's action affects others. His claim: if property rights are clearly defined and transaction costs (the costs of negotiating and enforcing deals) are zero, then bargaining between the parties leads to an efficient outcome, regardless of who initially holds the right. The noisy factory example shows this. A homeowner values quiet at 1,000 euros and insulation costs 700 euros. If the factory has the right to make noise, the homeowner pays 700 and the factory insulates. If the homeowner has the right to quiet, the factory would have to pay 1,000 in compensation, so it insulates for 700. Either way insulation is installed; only who pays whom changes.",
    "But transaction costs are never zero: there are information, bargaining and enforcement costs, strategic behaviour such as holdouts and misrepresentation, and problems with many parties. Even so, the lecture argues, we can design Coasean-inspired interventions that clarify rights and lower transaction costs so actors can negotiate, instead of just restricting. For Airbnb: tradable permits (a market for hosting nights), negotiations within homeowner associations (VvE) as collective bargaining, hosting taxes paid into a neighbourhood fund, and digital monitoring of platforms."

   "h": "Core assumptions and critiques",
   "p": [
    "The lecture closes with the core assumptions of rational choice theory: people act to maximise their self-interest (not only economic); this applies to all actors, from citizens to developers to municipalities; property rights and other institutions shape incentives and therefore strategies, and can be deliberately changed, though with possible side effects; collective action may be difficult when benefits are diffuse and individuals can free-ride; outcomes are evaluated as costs and benefits to actors; and knowledge is generally approached from a positivist stance, assuming universal rational behaviour and aiming at generalisable explanations (game theory, modelling, cost-benefit analysis). A warning: authors use these assumptions differently and not always consistently, so read each paper critically.",
    "On reproduction versus transformation, the lecture says rational choice can explain both, depending on the article. Arrangements are reproduced when actors use strategic positions to protect interests (insiders versus outsiders); transformation happens when actors deliberately redesign rules and incentives. Needham, quoted on the slide, writes that 'property rights can be deliberately changed in order purposefully to influence land use' (pp. 28–29 in the chapter); Taylor: landowner pressure led to modifications of the GAIC bill (p. 272). Five critiques end the lecture: an oversimplified model of human behaviour, ignoring power and inequality, too much focus on individual agency, limiting assumptions (like zero transaction costs), and a 'Nirvana fallacy in reverse'."
