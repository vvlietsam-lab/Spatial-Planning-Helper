    "Page numbers follow the original Road to Serfdom (1944), ch. 5, pp. 42–53, as printed in the reprint. The chapter opens with Adam Smith's warning that a statesman directing how people employ their capital assumes an authority no council could safely hold. Hayek defines the common feature of all collectivist systems as “the deliberate organisation of the labours of society for a definite social goal” (p. 42). Socialists complain that society lacks 'conscious' direction towards a single aim. Communism, fascism and other forms of collectivism differ in their goal, but all want to organise the whole of society and its resources for one unitary end and refuse to recognise autonomous spheres where individuals' ends are supreme. That, he says, is what 'totalitarian' truly means (p. 42).",
    "The lecture sets the context: after the Great Depression, which seemed to confirm Marx's predictions, and during the Second World War, many countries adopted some form of central economic planning. Hayek's question is whether such planning is compatible with democracy and individual freedom. His answer is no, and the chapter explains the mechanism step by step. The lecture presents him as the liberal counterpart to Marx: where Marx asks how to change the structure of ownership, Hayek asks what happens to freedom and democracy once the state tries to direct the economy as a whole."

   "h": "No complete ethical code",
   "p": [
    "The 'social goal' is usually described as the 'common good', 'general welfare' or 'general interest', but these terms are too vague to decide any concrete action (pp. 42–43). The welfare of millions cannot be measured on one scale of more and less; it depends on endless combinations of things and can only be expressed as a hierarchy of ends in which every need of every person has its place. A single plan therefore “presupposes, in short, the existence of a complete ethical code in which all the different human values are allotted their due place” (p. 43).",
    "We are not used to thinking of moral codes as more or less complete, because in a free society we constantly choose between values without a social rule telling us how. But if all means belong to society and are used according to one plan, a 'social' view must guide every decision, and our moral code would turn out 'full of gaps' (p. 43). Hayek adds a historical point: civilisation has been accompanied by a steady shrinking of the sphere governed by fixed rules. Morals have become fewer, more general, and increasingly mere limits around a sphere where individuals may do as they like; a comprehensive code for a unitary plan would reverse this. The essential point: no such code exists, and on most planning questions people have no views or conflicting views (p. 43)."

   "h": "The philosophy of individualism",
   "p": [
    "No mind could comprehend the infinite variety of competing needs of different people and weigh each one (p. 44). Whether a person is egoistic or altruistic is of minor importance: the basic fact is that nobody can survey more than a limited field or be aware of more than a limited number of needs. This, Hayek says, is the foundation of individualism, which “does not assume, as is often asserted, that man is egoistic or selfish, or ought to be”. Scales of value can exist only in individual minds, so only partial, different and often inconsistent scales exist. The individualist concludes that people should, within defined limits, follow their own values; the essence of the position is “this recognition of the individual as the ultimate judge of his ends” (p. 44).",
    "This does not exclude social ends. It limits common action to cases where individual ends coincide: 'social ends' are identical ends of many individuals, or ends people are willing to contribute to in return for help with their own. Often these are not ultimate ends but means that different people can use for different purposes; agreement is most likely where the common end is such a multi-purpose means (pp. 44–45)."

   "h": "The limits of the state's sphere",
   "p": [
    "An organisation formed for common ends, including the state, gets its own ends and means, but remains one 'person' among others, much more powerful yet with a limited sphere (p. 45). The limits of that sphere are set by how far individuals agree, and the probability of agreement necessarily decreases as the scope of action extends: some state functions have practical unanimity, others a substantial majority, and eventually there are 'almost as many views about what the government should do as there are different people'. The state can rely on voluntary agreement only where agreement exists.",
    "Moreover the state's sphere cannot be extended indefinitely while leaving the individual free elsewhere. Once the communal sector passes a certain share, its effects dominate the whole system. Hayek's example: in Germany as early as 1928, central and local authorities directly controlled about 53 per cent of national income, and thereby indirectly almost the whole economic life of the nation. Then almost every individual end depends on state action, and the 'social scale of values' guiding the state must cover practically all individual ends (p. 45)."

    "What happens when democracy embarks on planning that requires more agreement than exists (pp. 45–46)?    "h": "Why majorities fail and experts decide",
   "p": [
    "The fault lies not with representatives or parliament as such but with the task (pp. 47–48): assemblies are asked not to act where they agree but to produce agreement on everything. Majority decision works for choices between limited alternatives, but “it is a superstition to believe that there must be a majority view on everything”. Nor can a plan be voted clause by clause: 'An economic plan, to deserve the name, must have a unitary conception', and a complex whole cannot come from compromise. As with a military campaign, the task is delegated to experts. But a general has one supreme goal; an economic planner must choose between competing ends of different people, and only those who know all the facts can see which ends conflict. So “It is inevitable that they should impose their scale of preferences on the community for which they plan” (p. 48).",
    "Delegation is usually justified by technical complexity, but civil law is just as technical and nobody proposes delegating it, because it consists of general rules on which majority agreement is possible (p. 49). Hayek stresses that delegation of law-making as such is not the problem; delegating the power to make general rules, even to local authorities, can be sensible. The objectionable case is delegation because a matter 'cannot be regulated by general rules but only by the exercise of discretion in the decision of particular cases'; the authority then makes what are in effect arbitrary decisions, usually described as 'judging the case on its merits' (p. 49). A footnote on the Donoughmore Committee reads its demand for 'elasticity' as the conferment of arbitrary power (pp. 48–49)."

   "h": "From delegation to dictatorship",
   "p": [
    "Delegation to separate agencies is only a first step (pp. 49–50). Separate sectoral plans do not add up: 'Many separate plans do not make a planned whole' and may be worse than no plan. As long as the legislature keeps the vital decisions, nobody else can produce the comprehensive plan, so agreement that planning is needed plus parliament's inability to plan produce ever stronger demands to give the government or 'some single individual' power to act. The 'cry for an economic dictator' is a characteristic stage; Halévy saw it in a composite photograph of Percy, Mosley and Cripps.     "Planners reply that democracy stays intact as long as it keeps ultimate control; Mannheim argued sovereignty can be boundlessly strengthened by plenary powers without losing democratic control (pp. 50–51). Hayek sees a vital distinction. Parliament can control execution when it has agreed on the aim and only delegates detail. When delegation happens because there is no agreement on ends, the planning body chooses between ends whose conflict parliament does not even see, and parliament can only accept or reject the plan as a whole. Criticism remains a safety valve, but parliament 'cannot direct'; it is reduced to choosing persons with practically absolute power, tending towards 'plebiscitarian dictatorship', where a leader is periodically confirmed by a vote he can steer (p. 51)."

   "h": "The price of democracy and the limitation of power",
   "p": [
    "Hayek's positive principle: “It is the price of democracy that the possibilities of conscious control are restricted to the fields where true agreement exists, and that in some fields things must be left to chance” (p. 51). A centrally planned society cannot wait for majorities, so often the will of a small minority, the largest group able to agree, is imposed. Democracy has worked where a widely accepted creed limited government to fields where majority agreement was possible, and it is 'the great merit of the liberal creed' that it shrank the range of necessary agreement. If 'capitalism' means a competitive system based on free disposal over private property, then only within it is democracy possible; under a collectivist creed democracy will destroy itself (pp. 51–52).",
    "He refuses to make a fetish of democracy (p. 52). Unlike Acton's view of liberty, democracy is not the highest political end: “Democracy is essentially a means, a utilitarian device for safeguarding internal peace and individual freedom.” It is not infallible; there has sometimes been more cultural freedom under autocracy, and a homogeneous, doctrinaire majority could be as oppressive as a dictatorship. His claim is not that dictatorship must extirpate freedom, but that planning leads to dictatorship because dictatorship is the most effective instrument of coercion, which large-scale central planning needs. A democratic 'dictatorship of the proletariat' directing the economy would destroy personal freedom as completely as any autocracy. The final lesson: “it is not the source but the limitation of power which prevents it from being arbitrary” (p. 53). Power that cannot be guided by fixed rules becomes arbitrary, even if democratically conferred."
