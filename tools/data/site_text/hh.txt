    "Finnish cities own a lot of land (Helsinki owns 60% of its land area, p. 138) and traditionally allocated it free of charge for schools, day-care centres and libraries, and leased rather than sold land for development. Since the 1990s they increasingly sell land or charge market rent. Hyötyläinen and Haila call this 'entrepreneurial public real estate policy': a rent-maximising policy carried out not by private landlords, developers or REITs (real estate investment trusts) but by municipalities and the state (p. 137). It is neoliberal in the sense of introducing a market logic into the use of public land.",
    "The article asks three things (p. 137): how this policy was justified and why cities adopted a business model for public land; what conditions enabled it; and what kind of built environment it produced. The case is Eiranranta, a residential area on Helsinki's prestigious southern shore, where the city sold land to private developers who built a fenced neighbourhood for wealthy owners. The authors position themselves in debates on the political economy of urban land and neoliberal urban policy (Brenner and Theodore, Harvey, Peck), and claim three contributions: analysing land policy by the state and municipalities, analysing property and development rights, and analysing the inequalities neoliberal real estate policy produces in a Nordic welfare city (p. 138)."

   "h": "From leasing to selling: the new real estate policy",
   "p": [
    "Section 2 sets the background (p. 138). Leasing had given Finnish cities control over land use now and in the future, and keeping planning power with town planners helped create a socially sustainable, heterogeneous urban fabric without glaring concentrations of wealth or poverty. In Helsinki this changed in 1993, when the city began selling land. From 1994 the Real Estate Department managed all city land and started calculating 'shadow rents' for city services, which the authors describe as a way of getting used to the market game. In 2005 the city created the Premises Centre, which charges its own departments market rent.",
    "The state went first. Until 1995 a National Board of Public Building managed state property, funded from the budget; it was then replaced by state real estate companies (Kapiteeli, Senaatti, Sponda). The early-1990s recession was both an excuse and an opportunity: the state had taken over property from bankrupt banks and firms and adopted a new rationality of using public resources as efficiently as possible (p. 138, citing Haila 2008). The state companies then clashed with cities. One case: the state company demanded the price of developed land for land sold to Helsinki, while the city insisted on raw-land prices, because Finnish cities hold the 'planning monopoly' (sole right to assign development rights), so value increase from development belongs to them (p. 138)."

   "h": "Scaling down land use decisions",
   "p": [
    "Section 3 explains the enabling conditions: three changes gave cities a new environment for land decisions (pp. 138–139). First, a new state urban policy from the mid-1990s. Instead of financing all municipal services and reducing regional inequality, the Ministry of the Interior asked cities to draw up 'urban programmes' (modelled on British and French urban policy) and funded only some of them. This made cities compete for subsidies, encouraged partnerships with private actors and ceded planning responsibility to ad hoc project organisations and public-private partnerships. In Finland, the authors stress, it was the state that pushed cities towards new public management and competition (p. 139).",
    "Second, a new redistribution policy after the deep recession. The state cut spending, preached 'the necessity to save' and shifted social service provision to municipalities; the welfare state began turning into welfare cities (p. 139). Third, the Land Use and Building Act (MRL, 1999) gave cities more autonomy: their plans no longer needed state ratification. That removed the state's role as independent mediator between conflicting interests, a role it could no longer play anyway because it had become a real estate player itself. MRL also legalised 'development areas' and development contracts (agreements between a municipality and a private landowner about a plan and its implementation). Critics recalled that such contracts in the 1960s and 1970s produced poor-quality, peripheral housing estates. Selling land, the authors conclude, gave developers more power in planning (p. 139)."

   "h": "The Eiranranta deal and its motives",
   "p": [
    "Eiranranta was originally a rocky shore next to the old working-class district Punavuori, later used for light industry, waste water and a city depot; it was rezoned as residential in 2001 (p. 140). In 2004 the Real Estate Committee (REC, a politically elected body) decided to sell four sites, with the highest offer winning, provided bidders showed exceptional building expertise. This shows what 'efficiency' meant in practice: land should go to its highest-paying use. REC members explained the motives. The city budget contained massive sales goals for the Premises Centre. Deputy Mayor Pekka Korpinen, an economist, brought an economist's mindset and argued shorelines should be sold to maximise revenue rather than used for social housing. Even the name was strategic: 'Even the name was changed to Eiranranta with marketing in mind', linking it to the prestigious Eira district (p. 140).",
    "The deeper motive was competition. Land policy became a 'strategic' tool, and 'coordination' now meant competing with neighbouring municipalities rather than balancing development (p. 140). Helsinki feared that Espoo and Kauniainen would attract the 'good taxpayers', since municipal income tax is the main local revenue. The city also wanted to look like a 'global city' against Copenhagen, Stockholm and Tallinn, and here the authors explicitly borrow Harvey's (1989) terms: managerial strategies were being replaced by entrepreneurial ones (p. 140). Developers and firms insisted there was demand for luxury housing and that Anglo-American companies want to own, not lease, land (p. 141)."

    "The conclusions (pp. 143–144) return to the three contributions.
