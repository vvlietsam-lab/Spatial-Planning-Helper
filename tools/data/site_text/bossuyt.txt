    "The article starts from a blunt observation: the global housing crisis shows that private markets fail to guarantee adequate and affordable housing, and in response the 'commons' has emerged as a way of thinking about reclaiming housing (p. 1021). Ostrom's work on common-pool resources (fisheries, forests) studied stability extensively, but the urban literature has 'somewhat overlooked' this dimension (p. 1021).    "h": "Housing as a commons and the threat of enclosure",
   "p": [
    "The authors first define their terms. 'The commons' refers to resources that are collectively produced, shared and used for their use value; 'commoning' refers to the active practices of sharing and managing them (p. 1022). Two traditions meet here. Institutionalist scholars (Ostrom) see commons as sets of rules that communities create to manage resources without relying on state or market. Neo-Marxian scholars (Caffentzis and Federici, Dardot and Laval) see commoning as a political principle opposed to capitalism. The article builds on Massimo de Angelis, for whom commons are social systems of shared resources, sustained and reproduced by a group through commoning practices. Housing is not a commons by nature; it becomes one through an open-ended interaction of practices and rules.",
    "Housing commons have three dimensions (p. 1022). First, use value is prioritized over exchange value: housing is for living in, not for making profit, which the authors call a degree of 'decommodification'. Second, self-governance: residents themselves decide the collective-choice rules (who decides and how) and the operational rules (daily management). Third, solidarity and active participation grow through living together. Maintaining these three is a continuous struggle, because members, for example of limited-equity cooperatives, face a constant temptation of individual financial gain.",
    "The opposite of a commons is 'enclosure', the loss of commons leading to dispossession and market dependency (p. 1023). In housing, enclosure has three forms. Commodification happens when residents sell their homes individually for profit, as when a cooperative is dissolved. Marginalization happens when a self-selecting commons shuts out outsiders through exclusive use, membership or access. Cooptation happens when the state undermines self-governance, for example by slowly absorbing cooperatives into statist logics. In each case value ends up with a single party while the people who live in, rely on or work for the commons lose out. The authors add that Ostrom's principles, designed for natural resources threatened by overuse, risk depoliticizing housing if protection is seen only in material terms."

   "h": "The property debate and the analytical framework",
   "p": [
    "Theorists disagree on how commons should be protected, and the question of property sits at the heart of that disagreement (p. 1023). Dardot and Laval argue that commons cannot be objects of property at all, because property implies exclusion. Harvey, by contrast, considers 'some degree of closure' necessary to protect commons in a commodified world. De Angelis and Stavrides take a middle ground: commons survive through porosity (open, permeable boundaries) and proliferation (spreading beyond their own boundaries). Savini adds that fragile cooperatives rely on 'nesting and federating', creating networks of commons so that single projects do not become, in Vidal's phrase, 'islands in commodified waters' (p. 1023).",
    "Rather than deciding in theory whether commons and property are compatible, the authors propose to look at the concrete strategies people actually use to protect housing as a collectively managed, decommodified good (p. 1023). Their framework follows de Angelis' three-part view of a commons. The common good is the pooled resource, here understood through a relational concept of property: property as a bundle of claims, rights and obligations, shaped by legal frameworks. Such property relations can shield a commons, but they can also become a tool of enclosure when they take the 'ownership model' that vests power in one entity. The community consists of the commoners who make political claims about housing use. The practices are commoning itself: collective labor, decision-making, maintenance and cohabitation (pp. 1023–1024)."

    "The research is qualitative and longitudinal (p. 1024). Between 2016 and 2021 the team did participatory action research, which began because one team member worked as an architect on Dandara's retrofit plans. This follows the Brazilian model of 'extensão', in which public universities collaborate with societal actors such as social movements. The team attended assemblies, subcommittee meetings and site visits, and conducted 19 interviews: ten residents, three movement leaders, three policymakers, two social workers and an architect. Policy documents, laws and federal housing statistics complemented this material. The two cases were chosen precisely because they succeeded, so that the protective mechanisms that let them persist could be studied.",
    "São Paulo is marked by strong socio-spatial inequality and acute shortages (p. 1025). Public programmes long favored homeownership for formal-sector workers, so low-income groups built for themselves on the periphery. The democratization struggles of the 1980s produced the 1988 Constitution and the 2001 City Statute, which enshrined the right to housing and the 'social function of property'. Meanwhile, the city center emptied out: a 2022 census counted roughly 590,000 vacant properties against about 29,500 homeless families. Many low-income inner-city residents rent overcrowded tenements (cortiços), while the center itself has excellent infrastructure (pp. 1025–1026).",
    "The movement studied is the ULC (Unification of Tenement Struggles), founded in 1991 and part of the umbrella UMM (Union of Housing Movements), founded in 1987 (p. 1026). The UMM is organized in neighborhood 'origin groups' federated up to state, federal and international networks. Compared to some other movements, UMM and ULC are less confrontational and legitimize occupations through citizenship and social rights. Because prolonged occupations are precarious (a 2018 fire in an occupied building killed four people), the ULC has mostly shifted toward shorter, political occupations, although these can still last years (pp. 1026–1027)."

   "h": "The legal contradiction behind occupations",
   "p": [
    "Occupations exploit a contradiction in Brazilian law between two principles: the social function of property and the right to private property (p. 1027). The social function principle says that private property carries an obligation to be used in ways that benefit the collective; it restricts owners' rights for the social good and rests on a relational view of property associated with the legal scholar Léon Duguit. Because municipal governments are often entangled with local 'growth machines' (coalitions pushing real estate development), they implement this principle poorly, so movements take matters into their own hands.",
    "Movements therefore deliberately target vacant buildings: property that fails its social function. The UMM, for instance, chooses buildings whose owners have not paid taxes for long periods, or public buildings the state has neglected (p. 1027). Yet occupations remain disruptive, sometimes violent, and they infringe on a strongly valued principle. The authors add a point that becomes important later: many residents themselves aspire to homeownership, which in Brazil is seen as a precondition for full citizenship and a symbol of stability. Repression has also hardened; former president Bolsonaro called occupiers 'terrorists' (pp. 1027–1028)."

    "Dandara is a retrofit of a former state court in the Sé district, occupied in 2009 and renovated from 2014 to 2018; Marisa Letícia is a newly built complex on a former warehouse site in Brás, finished in 2019 (p. 1028).     "Appropriation. Occupiers force entry into vacant public buildings and immediately start caring for them: changing locks, cleaning, removing waste, setting rules of access (p. 1029). This care is also strategic, because any sign of degradation feeds hostile politicians. Residents insist that they occupy, not 'invade'; as one resident put it, 'we occupy a place where no one has it' (p. 1029). Legitimacy, following de Angelis, is a critical resource against eviction.",
    "Regularization. Movements then negotiate with the state for funding and formal status, using 'autogestão' (collective self-management) (pp. 1029–1030). Because movements lack money, they rely on the state. A federal ordinance (SPU 179) opened Dandara to negotiation, and Marisa Letícia entered the MCMV-Entidades programme, which for the first time gave movements direct access to federal housing funds. The federal cap of BRL 76,000 per unit was insufficient in the expensive center, so state and municipal top-ups were needed. The authors call this 'structural coupling': commons align with state systems to stabilize their claims (p. 1030).",
    "Renovation and moving in. Residents contributed labor ('mutirão') and joined committees for purchasing, security or finance (pp. 1030–1031). Units were allocated through an effort-based points system rewarding participation in meetings, occupations and marches, which prevented free-riding. One resident recalled: 'We built our social fabric over two years during the construction phase, getting to know one another so that by the time we moved in, we were no longer strangers' (p. 1031). Before move-in, residents guarded the sites day and night; afterwards, gates and doormen controlled access, and social workers taught courses on condominium life (p. 1032). The property outcomes differed: Marisa Letícia became an individual condominium with private titles and mortgages, while Dandara received an unusual use-right concession. In both, selling or subletting is forbidden by the movements, but they cannot enforce it. As the authors put it, 'Regularization, therefore, transforms collective use claims to buildings into state-sanctioned private legal claims' (p. 1033)."

   "h": "Five patterns of protection at three levels",
   "p": [
    "The core contribution is a typology of five patterns operating at three levels (pp. 1033–1034). Together they help maintain the three principles of a housing commons: use value over exchange value, self-governance and solidarity. At the level of the housing development itself, the patterns are regularization and restoration (p. 1034). Regularization is legal recognition, the main defense against eviction. But because cooperative or collective property regimes are uncommon in Brazil, regularization usually means condominium property with individual titles; out of necessity it may 'reproduce a hegemonic property regime'. Restoration is material: making the building habitable, livable and safe, including night vigils and physical barriers. Protection is thus both legal and physical.",
    "At the level between the development and the movement, the patterns are integration and ideation (pp. 1034–1035). Integration embeds residents in the wider housing struggle; the entidades (residents' organizations) are the entry point, and the points system encourages contribution, although it can clash with homeownership aspirations. Integrating new members also counters marginalization. Ideation, a term from Varvarousis, describes how commons ideas formed in one place later materialize elsewhere; the authors point to exchanges between Uruguayan housing cooperatives and Brazilian movements in the 1980s, and argue that technical know-how stays 'stored' in movements until a favorable political moment arrives.",
    "At the interface between movements and the state, the pattern is federation (p. 1035). Commoning is layered: floor committees, resident committees, entidades, movements and finally federations that lobby for political change. This echoes de Angelis' 'common ecologies'. Federation made it possible to insert autogestão into state policy, amending the market-led MCMV programme so that both projects could be built at all."

   "p": [
    "The authors stress that these protections are 'inherently partial' (p. 1035). Regularization and renovation convert public property, claimed collectively, into individual private titles. Private ownership plus individual mortgages turns households into 'indebted subjects' who could face foreclosure, and the conversion of collective political claims into personal debt relationships erodes collective action. Renovation also makes residents less dependent on the movement for tenure and habitability. So while regularization protects against eviction, it opens new avenues of enclosure; late in fieldwork, a unit appeared on a subletting platform (pp. 1035–1036).",
    "Theoretically, this means institutionalist approaches are 'only of partial use' because they miss the political dimension (p. 1036). The authors propose a relational perspective, patterns of protection, that follows how political claims and property regimes evolve together. Commoning appears as a pragmatic practice that takes different organizational forms over time and contains contradictions that should not be dismissed just because they do not fit an ideal type. Against earlier work stressing Brazilian public law (the City Statute), they highlight the mediating role of private law, concluding that 'Social rights help to create and reclaim housing as commons, but may not be enough to sustain them' (p. 1036)."
