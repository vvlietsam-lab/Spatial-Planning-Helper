   "p": [
    "Land value capture (LVC) means using public powers to recover part of the land value increases created by public decisions and infrastructure, for example by taxing uplift or requiring developers to contribute to infrastructure (p. 1804). Sorensen notes great variety worldwide: impact fees and tax increment finance in the US, planning gain in the UK, land readjustment, density bonuses, auctioned density permits in São Paulo, and huge differences in how much is captured, from Japan where most developments contribute nothing to the Netherlands where municipalities long captured all rural-to-urban uplift by buying land cheaply and selling serviced plots (p. 1804). Why such variation, and what shapes a jurisdiction's capacity to do LVC?",
    "He makes three contributions (pp. 1804–1805). First, LVC is at its core an attempt to purposefully shift the distribution of the costs and benefits of property development between public and private actors in the interest of social equity, and should be a key metric of the fairness of development. In Ontario the principle is that “growth should pay for growth” (p. 1804): buyers of new homes are on average wealthier than existing residents, so if development contributes nothing, lower-income residents subsidise either the newcomers or developers' profits. LVC rules are therefore important 'rules of the game' of capitalism, and contested.",
    "Second, he connects conjunctural analysis, an approach from Gramsci and Stuart Hall that reads how political, economic, social and ideological processes combine at a historical moment, with historical institutionalism (HI), which studies how institutions emerge and change over time (p. 1805). He argues HI adds midlevel concepts and methods for analysing contests over specific institutions: “Critical juncture analysis in this way helps to explain the processes through which political actors take advantage of conjunctural crises to further particular institutional changes” (p. 1805). Third, he applies this to Toronto, whose LVC has been contested since 1946 (p. 1805)."

   "h": "Two traditions of conjunctural analysis",
   "p": [
    "In critical urban studies, conjunctural analysis follows Stuart Hall's idea that crises arise when forces and contradictions in different practices and sites 'come together or con-join' in one moment (p. 1806). Peck's Transatlantic City papers revived it, calling for attention to contextual specificity and for 'midlevel' theory, but offered few details of method (p. 1806). Inch and Shepherd used it to analyse shifting planning ideologies in the UK housing crisis, and Leitner and Sheppard 'spatialised' it via land assetisation in Jakarta and Bangalore, but said nothing about specific institutional change (p. 1806). In all of these, the focus is on how hegemonic economic and ideological change affects local processes.",
    "HI, Sorensen argues, has long had robust concepts and methods for conjunctures and critical junctures (p. 1807). Most HI scholars see both as times when a confluence of events heightens the potential for major institutional change; Mahoney uses the terms interchangeably. The core definition, from Capoccia and Kelemen, is that critical junctures are “relatively short periods of time during which there is a substantially heightened probability that agents' choices will affect the outcome of interest” (p. 1807). They matter because of path dependence: institutions are hard to change, especially where 'positive feedback' reinforces them, and once an option is chosen it becomes progressively harder to go back (p. 1807)."

   "h": "The critical juncture method",
   "p": [
    "HI treats institutions as unequally distributive, produced by and constitutive of unequal power relations, and contested (p. 1807). Sorensen quotes North's 'rules of the game' and his own definition of planning institutions as 'collectively enforced rules and expectations' about the creation, management and use of urban space and property (p. 1807). For cities, it makes sense to focus on formal rules that shift costs and benefits of development between actors, like LVC, because they strongly affect land values and profits.",
    "Major change is analysed as a sequence (Figure 1, pp. 1807–1808). A crisis or confluence of events creates 'permissive conditions' that make institutions more open to change; during the critical juncture, actors fight for alternatives and a choice is made; a 'reactive sequence' follows in which the choice is contested and refined; eventually comes a period of relative stability. Following Soifer, permissive conditions raise the causal power of agency and contingency, while 'productive conditions' (engaged actors, political and legal structures, available change mechanisms, ideology, economics, and the timing of events) shape the specific outcome (pp. 1807–1808). Both are needed; neither alone is sufficient.",
    "Urban governance institutions are, he argues, especially prone to critical junctures: they sit in multi-level systems where senior governments can trigger change through legislation, disasters prompt new institutions, and urban systems face a constant stream of new challenges (p. 1808). He adds Fligstein and McAdam's 'Strategic Action Field', a meso-level order where actors interact under shared understandings of the field's purpose, power relations and rules (p. 1808). Those who benefit from an institution tend to defend it; those disadvantaged seek change."

   "h": "Evidence and cases: method and the Toronto setting",
   "p": [
    "The case is presented as a historical narrative using process tracing (following causal chains through a sequence of events) and reactive sequence analysis (p. 1808). It covers all significant changes to Ontario planning law, value capture and infrastructure finance since 1946, based on an exhaustive review of secondary literature and primary documents such as parliamentary debates and City reports, two sets of financial data on LVC revenues and Section 37 benefits (1998–2020), and eleven interviews in 2022–2023 with planners, councillors and consultants (pp. 1808–1809).",
    "Context matters. The Toronto metropolitan area grew from 1.1 million people in 1951 to 6.2 million in 2021 (p. 1809). Canadian provinces are constitutionally powerful, while municipalities are 'creatures of the province' with little legal autonomy; provinces can and often do unilaterally change planning law, boundaries and municipal taxation (p. 1809). Over 2009–2018 Toronto collected over C$490 million through 'Section 37' density agreements (extra density in exchange for community benefits) and C$2.29 billion in development charges (DCs, fees on new development similar to impact fees) (p. 1805)."

   "h": "Critical juncture 1: the Planning Act of 1946",
   "p": [
    "Three permissive conditions paved the way (pp. 1809–1810). Planning powers were weak: the 1917 Act let cities comment on subdivision plans but not require changes, contributions or even zoning, and a draft law with a betterment tax, proposed in 1916, was rejected. Infrastructure finance was a sore point: after Toronto stopped annexing suburbs in 1914, small suburban municipalities borrowed heavily in the 1920s, and ten of twelve could not service their debts in the Depression, forcing a provincial bail-out. And the war shifted attitudes towards intervention: the federal Curtis report recommended master plans, subdivision control and developers paying for streets and sewers, and the 1943 Ontario election crushed the Liberals and boosted the left (p. 1810).",
    "The Progressive Conservatives won a majority in 1945 and in 1946 passed a Planning Act that was far more limited than any proposal debated during the election (p. 1810). Still, it was the first law to give municipalities meaningful power over subdivision and zoning, a major limitation of property rights. Sorensen stresses contingency: no one expected the approach chosen. Crucially, the Act was ambiguous: it rejected making subdividers pay for services but gave local governments real discretion to refuse subdivision plans (only 42 percent of proposed lots approved in 1954–57), which gave suburbs bargaining power over developers (pp. 1810–1811).",
    "A reactive sequence followed. Metropolitan Toronto (a new upper tier of regional government) secured suburban infrastructure finance by requiring developers to provide on-site infrastructure and pay 'lot levies' for off-site works, which the 1946 Act never mentioned but subdivision powers made possible (p. 1811). After court challenges and appeals to the Ontario Municipal Board, the province amended the Act in 1959 to confirm the levies retroactively. This system lasted three decades (p. 1811)."

   "h": "Critical juncture 2: the Development Charges Act of 1989",
   "p": [
    "In the 1970s a suburban boom and new regional governments nearly doubled lot levies, and developers organised against them, arguing municipalities used them to keep property taxes artificially low (p. 1811). A 1983 revision added Section 36 (later 37), allowing extra density in return for local contributions, though this was little used until after 2000, but failed to settle levies, now called development charges. Municipalities (AMO) lobbied for DCs, developers (UDI, OHBA) opposed them, and a provincial working group could not agree (p. 1811).",
    "A Liberal minority government passed the Development Charges Act in 1989, giving clear statutory authority for DCs, linking them to building permits rather than subdivision plans, specifying which hard and soft services could be included and how to calculate charges, and letting school boards levy them too (pp. 1811–1812). Sorensen calls this a second critical juncture: it ended a long period of contention and launched a new legislative framework with its own reactive sequence (p. 1812).",
    "That sequence included a 1997 Conservative revision limiting DCs to hard infrastructure and requiring existing taxpayers to cover at least 10 percent, alongside amalgamation into a new City of Toronto and deep cuts to provincial transfers that caused a financial crisis (p. 1812). Under budget pressure and a construction boom, Toronto began collecting DCs in 2001 and used Section 37 far more: DC revenue grew from zero to almost C$700 million a year by 2020, and 697 Section 37 agreements yielded C$744 million in cash between 1998 and 2018 (p. 1812; Figure 2). Sorensen classes this as a change of practice within existing institutions, not a new critical juncture for LVC, even though amalgamation was one for governance (p. 1812)."

   "p": [
    "The Liberal government from 2003 supported intensification through a greenbelt and growth mandates but did not change DC or Section 37 rules (pp. 1812–1813). The Progressive Conservative government elected in 2018 aimed at deregulation and lower LVC revenue to boost housing supply. Bill 108 (2019) limited DCs and replaced Section 37 with a limited 'Community Benefits Charge'; Toronto protested that lower DCs would simply raise developer profits in a tight market (p. 1813). The continuing battles, with heavy lobbying by developers and municipalities, show that LVC is still intensely contested and that the main target is the rules of the game in provincial legislation (p. 1814)."

   "h": "Conclusions and what the author wants",
   "p": [
    "Sorensen's first conclusion is methodological: HI critical juncture analysis complements conjunctural analysis by specifying institutions, actors, change mechanisms and patterns, and by offering a comparative method (p. 1814). The Toronto pattern of critical juncture and reactive sequence is typical of planning laws created in response to crises. The ambiguity of 1946 about who pays for urbanisation is still at the centre of today's debate (p. 1814).",
    "Second, on path dependence: “In most cases path dependence is not a matter of 'lock-in,' as for many important institutions contestation is continuous and incremental change constant, but within a developmental pathway representing a relatively constrained range of options” (p. 1814). Critical junctures set the terrain of debate, which institutions are contested, the rules for changing rules, and the degree of ambiguity. In Fligstein and McAdam's terms, 1946 created a Strategic Action Field; even the recent radical changes alter amounts, timing and names, but keep density bonuses and DCs (pp. 1814–1815). Vancouver, by contrast, routinely captures about 70 percent of rezoning uplift, while Toronto planners aimed for 10–15 percent, now capped at 4 percent (p. 1815).",
    "Third, each juncture coincided with a housing crisis: the postwar shortage, the late-1980s price bubble and the current affordability crisis. Yet the first two strengthened LVC and the latest weakened it: “A housing crisis contributed to 'permissive conditions' in each case, but differing 'productive conditions' determined the outcome” (p. 1815). Variation in LVC between places is thus the product of contingent historical processes and the timing of change (pp. 1815–1816). He leaves open whether reduced LVC will lower housing prices or raise profits and land values, calling it a question for future research (p. 1815)."
