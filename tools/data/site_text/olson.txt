   "h": "What the chapter asks and why",
   "p": [
    "Hindmoor's textbook Rational Choice introduces key authors one chapter at a time; chapter 5 is about Mancur Olson's The Logic of Collective Action (1965). The topic is a basic puzzle of social life: people often need to act together to get something they all want, such as a union, a cleaner neighbourhood or lower emissions. The 'collective action problem', also called the free-riding problem, arises 'when it is better for all the members of a group that they act collectively even though it is in nobody's individual interest to contribute' (p. 102). Olson's famous statement is that 'unless the number of individuals is quite small, or there is coercion or some other special device to make individuals act in their common interest, rational, self-interested, individuals will not act to achieve their common or group interests' (Olson 1965: 2, quoted p. 102).",
    "Hindmoor's own position is set out at the start and repeated at the end: Olson is often read as saying free-riding makes collective action impossible or irrational. 'This is simply not the case' (p. 102). Olson says groups face a problem but recognises they may overcome it. The chapter therefore has two halves: first the problem, analysed with game theory; then the ways groups solve it, and finally the limits of Olson's self-interest assumption."

   "h": "Public goods and the logic of free-riding",
   "p": [
    "Olson begins by challenging the common assumption that people with shared interests will automatically work together for them (p. 102). The problem arises with goods whose benefits are non-excludable. Box 5.1 explains the classification economists use (p. 103). A good is excludable if its owner can stop others benefiting, and rivalrous if one person's use reduces what is left for others. This gives four types: public goods (non-excludable, non-rivalrous, like defence or a lighthouse beam), common-pool goods (non-excludable but rivalrous, like fishing grounds, which degrade beyond a certain use), toll goods (excludable, non-rivalrous, like toll roads) and private goods (both, like chocolate bars). Excludability is often a matter of cost. Because Olson defines public goods only by non-excludability, his problem applies to both public and common goods.",
    "The logic is simple. Each person reasons that their own contribution makes little difference and that they can benefit from others' contributions anyway. In Olson's words, all members share an interest in the benefit but 'have no common interest in paying the cost' (p. 104). If everyone reasons this way, there is no collective action. Hindmoor gives examples (pp. 104–105): workers who get pay rises whether or not they pay union dues; workers who would gain from revolution but let others risk the barricades; 'latent' groups like migrant workers who never organise despite vital shared interests, which undermines pluralists' claim that pressure groups represent the public; countries that all gain from lower CO2 emissions but each prefer growth; and states caught in a security dilemma."

   "h": "Game theory: the prisoners' dilemma",
   "p": [
    "Olson himself used no game theory, but Hindmoor uses it to clarify the argument (Box 5.2, pp. 106–107). Game theory studies choices whose outcomes depend on what others choose. Its standard assumptions: actors are instrumentally rational; there is common knowledge of rationality; actors choose between strategies such as 'cooperate' and 'defect'; they know the rules and pay-offs; and pay-offs can be ordinal (you know what is preferred, not by how much). Where there is no dominant strategy, players choose their best response to each other, producing a 'Nash equilibrium'; sometimes a shared 'focal point' helps coordination, like always meeting at Grand Central Station.",
    "In the prisoners' dilemma (pp. 105–109), two suspects, Jake and Keith, are held separately. Confessing while the other stays silent gives 1 year; both silent gives 2 each; both confessing gives 3 each; staying silent while the other confesses gives 4. Whatever Keith does, Jake is better off confessing, and vice versa, so confession is the 'dominant' strategy and both get 3 years, though both would prefer 2. Applied to a group deciding whether to join a protest (Figure 5.2, pp. 109–110): each person prefers to stay home while others protest, then all protesting, then all staying home, and least of all being alone at the barricades. Staying home is dominant, even though everyone cooperating is 'Pareto-superior' (better for everyone) to everyone defecting."

   "h": "Other shapes of the problem: assurance and chicken",
   "p": [
    "The prisoners' dilemma is not the only form (pp. 110–112). Imagine two farmers whose crops depend on maintaining a dam. If both must work a day for the dam to hold, each prefers mutual cooperation, is indifferent between the two outcomes where the dam fails, and least wants to work while the other stays home. This is the 'assurance' game: there is no dominant strategy, but since both prefer cooperation and know it, collective action should be easily achieved.",
    "If only one day of work is needed, which either farmer could do alone, the game becomes 'chicken' (p. 112). Each most prefers the other doing all the work, then sharing, then doing it all alone, and worst is nobody working and the dam collapsing. Again there is no dominant strategy. A farmer might try to pre-commit to defecting, for example by claiming a hurt hand, hoping the other gives in. But if both try this they reach the worst outcome, and in a single play 'anything can happen'. The point for planners: collective action problems differ in structure, and so do their solutions."

   "h": "Solutions 1: coercion and privileged groups",
   "p": [
    "Hindmoor notes that in reality collective action problems are often overcome: unions form, revolutions happen, Europe reduced sulphur dioxide emissions, and democracies do not go to war with each other (p. 113). It is tempting to say Olson was wrong, but that misreads him: 'Olson argues that there is a collective action problem. He does not suggest that groups will always fail to overcome it' (p. 113). The rest of the chapter reviews the solutions.",
    "The first is coercion. The state solves many problems by making us pay taxes for defence, roads and social security. Environmentalists invoked a coercive 'leviathan' for the 'tragedy of the commons' (Box 5.3, p. 114): Hardin's herders each keep adding animals to a shared pasture until it is ruined. Free-market economists instead blame missing property rights and propose privatisation or, for pollution, tradeable permits, drawing on the Coase theorem. Non-state actors can coerce too: Gambetta argues the Mafia prospered in a weak, mistrusted Southern Italian state by enforcing honest exchange for a cut (pp. 113–115).",
    "Second, privileged groups (pp. 115–116). In a latent group, nobody is willing to bear their share of the cost. In a privileged group, at least one member values the good so highly that they will pay the whole cost and tolerate others free-riding, like the flatmate who hates squalor and cleans alone. Hegemonic stability theory applies this internationally: a dominant power (Britain, later the US) maintains free trade and a reserve currency because it gains so much from that order. But if several members could each provide the good alone, the situation resembles chicken and the good may not be provided."

   "h": "Solutions 2: selective incentives, entrepreneurs and process benefits",
   "p": [
    "Third, selective incentives (pp. 116–117): benefits or punishments that go only to those who contribute or fail to. A union may offer free legal representation to members (positive), while workers may fear being ostracised, 'sent to Coventry', if they do not join (negative). But selective incentives cannot explain everything. Someone first had to form the union, and applying informal sanctions like ostracism is itself a collective good, creating a 'second-order' collective action problem and threatening an infinite regress. Leaders or entrepreneurs can break this: revolutions are rarely spontaneous, and entrepreneurs such as Trotsky, Castro or Martin Luther King can offer rewards and threats, perhaps motivated by future positions (p. 117).",
    "Fourth, process benefits (pp. 117–118). Olson treats contributing as a cost, but sometimes participating is itself rewarding, and then the problem 'simply dissolves'. Chong found that Civil Rights activists were driven partly by the adrenaline rush, the sense of making history, and being in fashion. On Israeli kibbutzim, where everyone is paid regardless of work, shirkers face vicious gossip, and there is no second-order problem because gossiping is enjoyable. Unlike separate incentives such as legal aid, process benefits are tied to the collective good itself: you cannot enjoy the rush of a demonstration without joining one (p. 118)."

   "h": "Solutions 3: conditional cooperation and group size",
   "p": [
    "Fifth, conditional cooperation in intermediate groups (pp. 118–121). Olson defines an intermediate group as one whose members can 'notice whether any other member is or is not helping to provide the collective good' (p. 118). There, people may cooperate as long as others do. In repeated games this can be rational. In Axelrod's tournaments, the simple strategy 'tit-for-tat' (cooperate first, then copy the other's last move) won twice, thanks to its simplicity, generosity and responsiveness, even though it never beats 'always defect' head to head (pp. 119–120). Ostrom's studies of fisheries, commons and irrigation show communities designing their own rules for allocation, monitoring and sanctions, without a leviathan or the market (p. 120).",
    "Conditional cooperation is most likely when (pp. 120–121): people expect to interact for a long time (trench soldiers in the First World War developed truces until commanders rotated troops); leaving the group is costly (kibbutzim); cooperation can be monitored cheaply (Valencia's irrigation system gives the next user an incentive to check the previous one); and groups are tightly knit, so ostracism bites. Olson links large groups to latency because it is harder to see who cooperates, but Hindmoor calls this 'at most, an empirical tendency' (p. 121): a mining community can be large and close, residents of a bed-sit small and anonymous. In iterated chicken, norms of sharing or taking turns may emerge, or one farmer may get trapped doing all the work (pp. 121–122). Box 5.4 adds reputation: a used-car dealer with a good name can be trusted not to sell a 'lemon' (pp. 122–123)."

   "h": "The limits of self-interest",
   "p": [
    "Olson assumes self-interest, yet many people contribute because a social norm tells them they ought to (pp. 122–123). Olson himself admits his analysis cannot explain 'non-economic' groups like Greenpeace or Amnesty International, which promote others' interests (p. 123). Experiments confirm this. Marwell and Ames let volunteers invest tokens in an individual bank (2 dollars each) or a group bank (5 dollars shared by all regardless of contribution), a one-shot prisoners' dilemma in which pure self-interest predicts zero group investment (p. 123). In fact, a substantial share of tokens went into the group bank. (Pages 124–125 of the scan, including Box 5.5 on norms, are largely unreadable, so details there are skipped; one legible finding is that when the individual bank's payoff was raised from two to five dollars, the share invested in the group bank fell from 40 to around 20 per cent, still well above zero.)",
    "Hindmoor then uses a threshold diagram (Figure 5.5, pp. 126–127). Even in a prisoners'-dilemma structure, once enough people cooperate (a threshold), cooperators may be no worse off than if all defected, so limited cooperation can be stable. But reaching that threshold from zero is hard, which is where a few unconditional cooperators, people acting on norms rather than self-interest, can make a significant difference. His summary: successful collective action does not usually require everyone to give up self-interest, but in its early stages 'it may however require that a few are prepared to do so' (p. 127)."

    "Hindmoor's assessment (pp. 127–128) is appreciative. The idea that groups must overcome a collective action problem seems obvious now only because Olson made it so; earlier thinkers like Hobbes, Hume and Rousseau had examples, but Olson offered the first general analysis of why and when the problem arises. Rational choice theorists could study these, but not only they; a fuller theory would draw on social psychology on group identity and on political science findings that 'insider' groups in policy communities have more influence."
