    "p": [
     "The promise is broad (p. 1). Planning support systems (PSS), urban analytics and city information modelling have enabled a transformative digitalisation of planning; newer tools include digital twins, AI, virtual and augmented reality, gamification platforms, public participation GIS (PPGIS) and the Internet of Things. Some tools collect citizen feedback or help understand problems; others support interaction between authorities, professionals, firms and citizens. Social media (Twitter, Facebook, Weibo) let civil society and marginalised groups voice their views.      "The problems are just as real (p. 1). Technology supply and planning demand often do not match, interfaces are user-unfriendly, and tools carry socio-political bias. Digital tools can reshape power relations and create new inequality and exclusion. Planning authorities and universities have a large digital skills gap, integration is uneven and fragmented, and there are new risks such as the digital divide and cybersecurity. Hence the editorial's central warning: \"Digitalization of planning is not just the straightforward implementation and use of new technologies\" (p. 1). It demands new ways of planning, proper methods and adjusted communication, and a two-way exchange between academia and practice."

    "h": "Digital planning versus Planning Support Science",
    "p": [
     "Section 2 separates digital planning from the older field of planning support systems (p. 2). Britton Harris defined PSS in 1989, arguing that GIS could loosely integrate computer techniques to generate, evaluate and test plans. Since then PSS have been understood as computer-based tools that help planners and stakeholders with scenario building, forecasting, participation and trend analysis, evolving from GIS, land-use models and decision support systems towards digital twins, AI, VR and data infrastructures. As the field matured, Geertman and Stillwell proposed reading PSS as Planning Support Science, echoing Klosterman's point that \"planning support should start from the peculiarities of the planning activity itself, and not from the characteristics of the technology\" (p. 2).",
     "The relationship is layered (p. 2). PSS are tools that can be used within digital planning. Planning Support Science is scientific: it asks how instruments can be fitted to the governance and application at hand in a specific context. Digital planning asks the follow-up question of how those instruments contribute to inclusive and effective planning, and is application-oriented, focused on results in practice. One is a prerequisite for the other. Delivering it requires a 'quadruple' collaboration between government, market parties, societal organisations and individuals, and education and research institutes."

    "h": "Smart cities and platform urbanism",
    "p": [
     "Section 3 compares digital planning with two other concepts (pp. 2–3). The smart city debate is younger; it started academically with Giffinger et al.'s six smart city domains (2007) but was driven mainly by business. Technology companies built entire smart city ecosystems, such as Songdo in South Korea; Barcelona, Amsterdam and Helsinki are often cited as European best practice. The concept was long fuzzy; one durable definition (Caragliu et al.) links investment in human and social capital and infrastructure to sustainable growth, quality of life and participatory governance. Critics stress its technocratic nature and weak link to the real problems of citizens.",
     "Platform urbanism is 'a mode of urbanization that is deeply shaped by the conditions and affordances of platforms' (Barns), driven by smartphones and 4G/5G (p. 2). It includes everyday platforms (Uber, Airbnb, Alibaba, Facebook) and government and business initiatives such as Mobility-as-a-Service or Alibaba's City Brain traffic platform. The authors conclude that digital planning includes both as potential tools, but differs in being strategic, procedural and operational, more government-led and more consciously guided by policy goals of sustainable and inclusive development (pp. 2–3). Because smart city applications and platforms often develop without any planning intervention, poor alignment can hinder digital planning, so data governance becomes critical."

    "h": "A definition and three principles",
    "p": [
     "Section 4 reviews definitions (p. 3). Potts and Webb see digital planning as integrating ICT into engagement, decision-making, daily practice and strategy. Hersperger et al. find that digitalisation in land-use planning makes administrative processes more efficient and supports government-citizen interaction and transparency, but does less for innovation. Nummi et al. show that dominant planning styles and institutions can block technological innovation, so digital transformation needs cultural change in organisations. Batty and Yang propose a cyclic system with two loops: an evidence analytics loop driven by data, and a democratic decision-making loop of argument, discussion, design and politics.",
     "The authors then define digital planning as \"the application of digital technologies and data-driven approaches to enhance efficiency, effectiveness, and inclusivity in planning processes at different scales to improve social, economic, and environmental outcomes for achieving a more sustainable urban future\" (p. 3). Each term is explained. Efficiency means efficient use of resources and faster, better-coordinated decision-making, including real-time monitoring. Effectiveness means better strategies and policies based on data analytics and prediction, and wider support through participation. Inclusivity means participation, engagement and empowerment, including marginalised groups in formal and informal channels."

In Finland, Nummi and Hapuoja found a technology-driven culture dominating national development, though co-development benefited both developers and planners. Dane et al. used immersive VR to co-design healthier spaces in Eindhoven, with mostly positive participant experiences. Flacke et al. co-designed a mapping tool with people with disabilities in Zwolle and argue that digital, physical, social and procedural accessibility are all needed. The authors suggest a future typology should classify approaches by purpose, users, level of digitisation, type of technology, level of complexity and environment (analog, VR/AR, metaverse), warning that \"Any typology should also encompass more than the narrow operational objectivity of digital planning as a set of tools\" (p. 4)."

    "h": "Recommendations",
    "p": [
     "The conclusion is that realising digital planning requires more than technology adoption (p. 4). Tools should be co-developed with planners, citizens and technical experts, and research should explore how to scale co-design from experiments to widespread practice. Attention is needed to power: how existing structures shape tool design and how new tools reshape those structures. Governance must adapt through institutional reform, new policies, a culture supportive of innovation, training of officials, and digital participation. Citizens and marginalised groups should be engaged in formal and informal processes, supported by uniform data standards and integration of social sensing data and local knowledge.",
     "Two further warnings stand out (pp. 4–5). AI is shifting from a tool that supports humans to an autonomous, 'agentic' decision-maker, raising concerns about democratic representation, ethics, privacy and justice, especially as politicians with tight budgets may be tempted to substitute human-centred processes with AI tools. Governance should keep AI transparent, inclusive and human-centred, while not forgetting 'low-tech' tools such as social media. Finally, planners need digital literacy and interdisciplinary skills, curricula should cover participatory technology, data analytics and digital ethics, and a systematic review of definitions should prevent the conceptual fragmentation that weakened the smart city concept."
