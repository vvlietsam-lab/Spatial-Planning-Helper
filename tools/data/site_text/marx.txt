   "h": "What the text asks and why",
   "p": [
    "Note: this is an online text (Marxists Internet Archive) without page numbers, so no page references are given. 'Estranged Labour' is a section of Marx's First Manuscript of 1844; the manuscript breaks off at the end. Marx starts by accepting the premises, language and laws of political economy (the classical economics of his day): private property, the separation of labour, capital and land, wages, profit and rent, division of labour and competition. Using its own terms, he shows that the worker sinks to the level of a commodity, 'the most wretched of commodities', that the worker's wretchedness grows with the power of his production, that competition concentrates capital in few hands, and that society splits into two classes: property owners and propertyless workers.",
    "His complaint is that political economy describes these laws without explaining them. “Political economy starts with the fact of private property; it does not explain it to us.” It treats competition as accidental and presents greed and the war among the greedy as the only driving forces, without grasping their inner connection. Marx rejects the economists' habit of explaining things by inventing a fictitious 'primordial condition', which only pushes the question back, like theologians explaining evil by the Fall. Instead: “We proceed from an actual economic fact”, and trace the connections between private property, greed, the division of labour and the money system."

   "h": "The central fact: the worker grows poorer as wealth grows",
   "p": [
    "Marx's starting fact: “The worker becomes all the poorer the more wealth he produces, the more his production increases in power and size.” The worker becomes an ever cheaper commodity the more commodities he creates, and “The devaluation of the world of men is in direct proportion to the increasing value of the world of things.” Labour produces not only commodities; it produces itself and the worker as a commodity.",
    "The key concept is objectification. “The product of labor is labor which has been embodied in an object, which has become material: it is the objectification of labor.” In itself this is how humans realise themselves. But under the conditions political economy describes, this realisation appears as loss of realisation for the worker; objectification as loss of the object and bondage to it; appropriation as estrangement, as alienation. The product confronts the worker as something alien, a power independent of him. Marx compares this to religion: the more man puts into God, the less he retains in himself. The more the worker produces, the less he can possess and the more he falls under the dominion of his product, capital.",
    "Nature enters here. The worker can create nothing without the sensuous external world, which provides both the material for labour and the means of physical subsistence. Under estrangement, the more the worker appropriates nature through labour, the more he loses these means of life in a double sense: nature stops being the object of his own labour, and stops directly supplying his subsistence. He becomes a servant of his object: he receives work, and through work the means to survive, first as a worker and only then as a physical being."

   "h": "Estrangement in the act of production",
   "p": [
    "Estrangement shows not only in the result of labour but in production itself; the product is only the summary of an activity that is itself 'active alienation'. Marx asks: “What, then, constitutes the alienation of labor?” First, labour is external to the worker: he does not affirm but denies himself in it, feels unhappy rather than content, and does not freely develop his physical and mental energy. “The worker therefore only feels himself outside his work, and in his work feels outside himself. He feels at home when he is not working, and when he is working he does not feel at home.” Hence: “His labor is therefore not voluntary, but coerced; it is forced labor.” It is not the satisfaction of a need but only a means to satisfy needs outside it, and it belongs to another, not to the worker.",
    "The result is an inversion of human and animal. The worker “only feels himself freely active in his animal functions – eating, drinking, procreating, or at most in his dwelling and in dressing-up, etc.; and in his human functions he no longer feels himself to be anything but an animal.” Marx adds that eating and drinking are genuinely human functions too, but when abstracted from other human activity and turned into sole, final ends, they become animal. The HC4 lecture quotes this passage together with the later sentence on wages. This second aspect, estrangement from one's own activity, Marx calls self-estrangement, as opposed to the estrangement of the thing discussed before."

   "h": "Species-being and the estrangement of man from man",
   "p": [
    "The third aspect needs Marx's notion of 'species-being'. “Man is a species-being”: humans treat their own species, and nature universally, as objects of consciousness and will, and relate to themselves as a universal and free being. Physically humans live from nature like animals, but their universality shows in making all of nature their 'inorganic body': “Nature is man's inorganic body – nature, that is, insofar as it is not itself human body.” Humans live from nature and must remain in continuous exchange with it.",
    "The contrast with animals is consciousness. “The animal is immediately one with its life activity. It does not distinguish itself from it.” Humans make their life activity itself an object of will and consciousness: “Conscious life activity distinguishes man immediately from animal life activity.” An animal “produces only under the dominion of immediate physical need, whilst man produces even when he is free from physical need and only truly produces in freedom therefrom”; man 'also forms objects in accordance with the laws of beauty'. Estranged labour reverses this: “in degrading spontaneous, free activity to a means, estranged labor makes man's species-life a means to his physical existence.” What makes us human becomes a mere means of survival.",
    "The fourth consequence follows: “An immediate consequence of the fact that man is estranged from the product of his labor, from his life activity, from his species-being, is the estrangement of man from man.” When a person confronts himself, he confronts other people; each relates to others according to the estranged position in which he finds himself as a worker. Estrangement is therefore not only a private experience but a social relation."

   "h": "Who owns the product? Private property and wages",
   "p": [
    "If the product and the activity are alien to the worker, to whom do they belong? Not to the gods, and not to nature. “The alien being, to whom labor and the product of labor belongs, in whose service labor is done and for whose benefit the product of labor is provided, can only be man himself.” If the worker's activity is a torment to him, 'to another it must give satisfaction and pleasure': someone outside production who becomes master over it. Through estranged labour the worker thus produces the relation of a stranger, the owner, to labour.",
    "This yields Marx's reversal of political economy: “Private property is thus the product, the result, the necessary consequence, of alienated labor, of the external relation of the worker to nature and to himself.” Political economy treats private property as the starting point and cause; analysis shows it is the consequence, although later the relation becomes reciprocal: private property is both the product of alienated labour and the means by which labour alienates itself. For the same reason “wages and private property are identical”: wages are a direct consequence of estranged labour. So “An enforced increase of wages … would therefore be nothing but better payment for the slave, and would not win either for the worker or for labor their human status and dignity.”",
    "Politically, “the emancipation of society from private property, etc., from servitude, is expressed in the political form of the emancipation of the workers”, because the whole of human servitude is bound up in the worker's relation to production. Marx then sets two tasks: to determine the general nature of private property in relation to truly human property, and to explain how man comes to alienate his labour and how this is rooted in human development. The manuscript announces that the relation of the non-worker (the person alien to labour) to the worker and to labour will be considered next, and then breaks off."

   "h": "How the HC4 lecture frames Marx",
   "p": [
    "The 17 September lecture places this text in a wider reading of Marx, much of which is not in 'Estranged Labour' itself. It presents Marx as adapting Hegel's dialectic: a deterministic conception in which human beings are 'subject to rather than actors of' broad societal change, with Hegel's motor of history, 'the Spirit', replaced by the means of production and the opposition between 'owners' and 'disowned'. Human significance comes from our transformative relationship with nature through labour, which property rights disturb; the industrial revolution alienated workers from their product, turning them from people transforming matter for their own use into sellers of their bodies for a small wage. The slides add materialism's base (means and relations of production) and superstructure (values, norms, ideology).",
    "Politically the lecture draws the line to revolution: change history by changing the base, abolish private property, and pass from a capitalist state via a socialist state to a stateless society (quoting Lenin). Marx's value is material equality through shared ownership of the means of production, set against Hayek's liberty and equality of rights. For planning, the 'progressive planner' slide quotes Madden and Marcuse (2016): unaffordable and overcrowded housing are “not market failures—they are how the market works”, so cities need decommodified housing such as public or cooperative homes. The check-in questions ask whether structure or agency dominates in Marx and whether change is emergent or deliberate."

   "h": "Conclusions and what Marx wants",
   "p": [
    "Within this text Marx's conclusion is analytical and political at once. Estranged labour, not private property, is the root; private property is its product. Wage rises or equal wages within this system cannot restore human dignity to labour; emancipation requires ending private property, expressed politically as the emancipation of the workers. The text is openly unfinished: Marx sets himself questions about the origin of alienation in human development and about the relation of the non-worker to the worker, and the manuscript stops. The broader programme of historical materialism and revolution that the lecture describes belongs to Marx's later work and to the lecturers' framing, so distinguish carefully in the exam between what this text says (four forms of estrangement, private property as consequence) and the lecture's wider Marxism."
