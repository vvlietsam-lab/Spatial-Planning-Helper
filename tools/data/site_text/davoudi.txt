   "h": "What the article asks and why",
   "p": [
    "Davoudi writes in 2006, when 'evidence-based policy' had become a buzzword in Britain. After New Labour's 1999 White Paper on Modernising Government, which asked for 'better use of evidence and research in policy-making', government spending on policy research rose sharply: over 350 million pounds in 1998/99 alone (p. 14). The idea spread to other English-speaking countries and to the EU, for example through the ESPON research programme on European spatial planning. Davoudi opens with a play on words: evidence used to be part of criminal conviction; now it seems to be part of political conviction too, 'or so we are led to believe' (p. 14).",
    "Her question is what this enthusiasm for evidence actually rests on, and whether it matches how planning and policy really work. Her answer, stated in the abstract, is that much of the drive is rooted in a 'linear and utilitarian' view of research, built on three misconceptions: about the nature of evidence, the role of experts, and the influence these can have on policy (p. 14). Her second claim is historical: these assumptions are very similar to those of positivist, rational planning in the 1960s and 1970s. She uses British planning examples to show the gap between the 'ideal' and the 'real' world of using evidence."

   "h": "Two models of how research relates to policy",
   "p": [
    "Davoudi distinguishes two views of the policy-research interface (p. 15). The 'instrumental' model assumes the relation is unproblematic, linear and direct. It has two versions. Either research leads policy (policy is evidence-driven), which Young et al. call the expert 'on top'; or research follows policy (research is policy-driven), where 'the expert is 'on tap', but not on top' (p. 15). Both are linear and 'differ only in the posited direction of influence' (Fig. 1). The late-1990s British push was mostly the on-tap version: evidence must be quick, intelligible and available on demand, summed up by the slogan 'What matters is what works', with little attention to what works for whom and in what circumstances.",
    "Research for policy's sake is also selective: areas not defined as policy problems get no research money. Davoudi's example is strategic waste planning, where a House of Commons report found that nobody knew where hazardous waste was going (pp. 15–16). The alternative is the 'enlightenment' model (p. 16). Here research has indirect, slower benefits: it does not produce 'punchy policy messages' but illuminates the landscape in which decisions are made, deepening understanding of when interventions might work and informing public debate. This is 'evidence-informed' rather than 'evidence-based' policy."

   "h": "Three misconceptions behind the instrumental view",
   "p": [
    "The current enthusiasm, Davoudi argues, is dominated by a utilitarian view of research resting on flawed assumptions about how policy handles evidence, where evidence is mostly understood as scientific research (p. 16). She summarises the three misconceptions, admitting some oversimplification: 'policy-making is a rational process, evidence can only be generated through positive science, and experts are apolitical and value-free – and they know best' (p. 16).",
    "Each misconception matches one element of the rational model. The first concerns the policy process (it is treated as rational and linear rather than political). The second concerns the nature of evidence (only positivist science counts, not experiential or tacit knowledge). The third concerns experts (they are neutral technicians standing outside politics). She then shows that the same three assumptions were made in the positivist heyday of planning, so the 'new' evidence-based agenda repeats an old pattern and ignores its known problems."

   "h": "Evidence in the ideal world: from Geddes to systems planning",
   "p": [
    "Davoudi traces a short history of evidence in British planning (pp. 16–18). Using descriptive data is one of planning's oldest practices: Charles Booth's 1887 social survey of London helped trigger the public health and housing acts. Patrick Geddes's 'survey before plan', later 'survey-analysis-plan', became every planning student's rule. Following Taylor, she notes three shortcomings: there was no stage of problem definition, so goals were left to intuitive experts seen as guardians of the public interest; it produced one single plan rather than alternatives; and the process ended with the plan, with no learning, implementation or updating (pp. 16–17). Geddesian planning was linear and instrumental, but its evidence was only physical description, and the planner was a creative visionary who made a leap from survey to plan. Plans were really based on intuition and aesthetic ideas of urban form.",
    "A 'paradigm shift' came in the 1960s with two theories: the systems view of cities (from cybernetics, via McLoughlin and Chadwick) and planning as a rational decision process (p. 17). Planning became systems analysis and control, requiring analytical rather than descriptive evidence, drawn from location theory, logical positivism and Popper's idea of falsifiable hypotheses. The 1948 Schuster Committee opened the profession to geographers, economists and sociologists; computers and spatial interaction models promised to turn planning from art into science (p. 18). The rational process had five steps: define problems and goals, identify alternatives, evaluate them, implement, and monitor. But, citing Lindblom, it hardly matched the 'disjointed, incremental' way decisions were really made."

   "h": "The link: rational planning then, evidence-based policy now",
   "p": [
    "This is the hinge of the article (p. 18). The assumptions of rational comprehensive planning in the 1960s and 1970s 'bear a strong resemblance' to those of today's instrumental view of evidence-based policy. Both treat policy-making and planning as sciences using value-free techniques to solve well-defined, neatly structured problems. Both suffer from what Nancy Shulock calls 'the paradox of policy analysis': the mismatch between how the policy process should work and its messy, uncertain, unstable and political reality.",
    "Davoudi then turns to the 'real world' (p. 18). Following Ball, policy-making is 'bricolage': borrowing bits of ideas, copying, cannibalising theories and fashions, producing ramshackle compromises that are reworked in practice. More information does not automatically help: it can confuse choices that would be easier in relative ignorance. She quotes Keynes that 'there is nothing a government hates more than to be well-informed' (p. 19), because it makes decisions harder. And much research, even when government pays for it, simply sits on the shelf; a Cabinet Office audit found little commissioned research was actually used (p. 19)."

   "h": "Weiss's four competitors to evidence",
   "p": [
    "Evidence is not the only influence on policy. Davoudi uses Carol Weiss's 'four Is': ideology, interests, institutional norms and practices, and prior information (p. 19). Ideology: basic values can shape policy so strongly that no data changes it. British urban containment and the Green Belt are her example: sustained by the values of (some would say) middle-class suburban property owners, even after Peter Hall's major 1973 study showed its regressive effects, giving more to those who had most (p. 19). The Barker Review of housing supply reopened the issue in 2004. Interests: organisations use evidence to justify decisions already made. DEFRA defined evidence as any information that helps turn its strategic priorities into something concrete (p. 19). Young et al. call this the political or tactical model, marked by cherry-picking; Thatcher-era think tanks gave 'intellectual legitimacy' to decided policies.",
    "Institutions: organisational traditions, culture and capacity decide how research is communicated and understood (p. 20). Demand for quick fixes discourages in-depth research; the sheer volume of reports overwhelms policy makers; there is a shortage of 'intelligent users' of research; and politicians rarely read long reports. Prior information, which Davoudi widens to the nature of evidence itself (p. 20): facts become evidence only when used to support or refute a proposition; evidence includes formal and informal, expert and experiential, systematic and tacit knowledge; what counts is what is available and accessible at a given time and place; and validity concerns the link between facts and claims. Evidence is often incomplete or contradictory: the debate on high-density housing is unresolved, yet policy already promotes it (pp. 20–21)."

   "h": "Power, uncertain science and the 'sound plan'",
   "p": [
    "New evidence does not enter a 'pristine environment'; it must fit into policy makers' understanding of the world, which comes from science but also anecdote and tacit knowledge (p. 21). Policy is as much about power and agenda-setting as about finding truth. Quoting Flyvbjerg, knowing what decides whether knowledge 'gets to count' is as important as the knowledge itself. And science itself is uncertain: Popper wrote that 'Science does not rest upon solid bedrock' (p. 21). Her summary line borrows from Solesbury: 'there is more to policy and practice than the disinterested pursuit of truth and wisdom' (p. 21). Creativity, imagination, mobilisation and political support are also needed; British planning in the 1980s and 1990s lacked exactly these, becoming a bureaucratic routine where evidence meant 'proper surveys'.",
    "Her final example is the 2004 planning reform, which introduced a test of 'soundness' for spatial plans (pp. 21–22). PPS12 required an up-to-date evidence base and an independent examination of whether strategies rest on 'robust and credible evidence'. Davoudi argues this uses evidence in a quasi-judicial sense, not very different from the old 'reasoned justification'. Early signs showed the test was largely procedural, a tick-box exercise about the process followed rather than the merits of sites. Substantive choices, such as one growth strategy versus another, are argued out through different frames of reference, not through the scientific validity of technical evidence (p. 22)."

   "h": "Conclusions and what the author wants",
   "p": [
    "Davoudi concludes (p. 22) that the enthusiasm for evidence-based policy comes from an instrumental view with much in common with positivist planning. Both rest on 'ambitious and naïve assumptions' that complex political and socio-economic processes can be made technical and controlled through a scientific process. The 1970s structure plans had huge statistical reports of survey but lacked innovation and 'imagination'. Then, in the 1980s and 1990s, the debate focused so much on the planning process (knowing how, for example communicative planning) that the substantive knowledge of how cities work (knowing what) was neglected.",
    "What she wants is not less evidence but a different relationship. Following the RTPI's 2003 call for critical thinking about space and place, she argues for keeping the broader enlightenment model: research that informs policy rather than trying to 'spoon-feed politicians', and a two-way relationship in which policy and research agendas are set through interaction (p. 22). Narrow instrumentalism both limits imagination and rests on fragile assumptions. Hence 'less emphasis on evidence-based policy' and more on 'an evidence-informed society' and the capacity to use evidence (p. 22). The article is a conceptual essay with British examples, not an empirical study, so it does not test how often each model occurs."
