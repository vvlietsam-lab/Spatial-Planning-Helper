    "h": "What the article asks and why",
    "p": [
     "Petter Næss wrote this paper in 2000, when 'sustainable development' had become part of everyday planning vocabulary but planning theory itself hardly engaged with it. His starting observation is that there were many texts about the physical shape of sustainable cities, and many texts about planning procedures, but very few that connected the two. Procedural planning theory (theory about how planning should be done: who participates, how decisions are reached) tended to stay detached from the substance of what planning deals with (p. 503). For sustainability, Næss argues, that separation is especially inappropriate, because the right procedure depends on the goal. If the goals fit local residents' lifestyles, bottom-up planning may work; if those lifestyles are themselves a main part of the problem, it will not (p. 504).",
     "The paper therefore does two things in one argument. First it asks what a sustainable urban development would substantively require in a wealthy country. Then it asks which planning procedures could deliver that, and which cannot. His answer to the first question is demanding (much less energy use, much less land take, and an end to growth in floor space per person). His answer to the second is critical of the dominant communicative and consensus-based planning theory of the 1990s, and ends in a plea for goal-oriented planning and alliance-building."

    "h": "A concept at risk of being watered down",
    "p": [
     "Section 1 is a warning about the word itself. Because so many strategies and projects now claim to follow from sustainable development, Næss turns the Norwegian saying around: 'a pet name gets many children' (p. 504). His diagnosis is sharp: 'It has become politically impossible not to be a supporter of a sustainable development, so there is a clear danger that the concept will be watered out' (p. 504).",
     "He reads the Brundtland discourse as originally a counter-discourse to the dominant growth discourse in industrial countries. A dominant discourse can defend its hegemony by de-radicalising and redefining the challenger (Van Dijk), and Næss sees attempts to stretch sustainable development to cover all kinds of extra concerns as an example of that (p. 504). In a note he makes this concrete: Campbell's (1996) triangle places economic growth as one pole of sustainability on the same level as equity and environment, whereas Brundtland treated growth only as a possible means, and only if its content changes (note 4, p. 520). So Næss deliberately sticks to a narrow, Brundtland-based reading."

    "h": "What Brundtland actually demands",
    "p": [
     "Section 2 sets out that reading. Sustainable development combines ethical norms of welfare, distribution and democracy with the recognition that nature's capacity to absorb human pressure is limited (p. 504). The core is meeting everybody's basic needs (food, water, clothes, shelter, work, energy, hygiene), in poor and rich countries, now and in the future, without jeopardising the natural systems life depends on, and through democratic and legitimate decisions.",
     "Næss draws out two implications (p. 505). Present generations must limit their encroachments on nature and their use of non-renewable resources. And if poor countries need more material consumption to meet basic needs, rich countries must consume less so that the total environmental load stays within limits. Sustainable development therefore contains a strong element of distributive ethics: fairness across generations and across space (p. 505). He also notes that Brundtland mentions nature's intrinsic value and demands democratic procedures, support for grassroots initiatives and stronger local democracy, echoed in Agenda 21. Keep this last point in mind: later he will argue that it does not mean handing all decisions to local communities."

    "h": "Five substantive criteria for sustainable spatial planning",
    "p": [
     "Section 3 translates the concept into criteria for land use, buildings and infrastructure. Næss stresses that the task differs by context: in poor countries improving housing and hygiene is central, while in wealthy regions the key task is to reduce the per capita use of natural and environmental resources (p. 505). From the literature he distils five elements: (1) cut energy use and emissions per person to a level compatible with global ecological and distributional criteria; (2) minimise conversion of natural areas, ecosystems and farmland; (3) minimise environmentally harmful building materials; (4) replace open-ended resource flows with closed loops relying more on local resources; (5) a healthy urban environment with enough green space for people to experience nature (p. 506).",
     "He argues each point briefly. On energy, the UN climate panel's suggested 60% global cut in CO2 would, with a rise in consumption in developing countries, require industrial countries to cut considerably more than 60%; renewables alone cannot do it, so a 'low-energy future' is needed (p. 506). Farmland matters because a growing world population will limit Europe's food imports (pp. 506–507).",
     "He then notes that the criteria can conflict because they reflect two focal points: 'ecology within the city' (parks, clean air and water for residents' health) and the 'city in ecology' (the city as part of the larger ecosystem, with ecological footprints far beyond its hinterland) (p. 507). Brundtland, he says, requires planners to add the second perspective to the first."

    "p": [
     "Section 4 compares the competing urban models. In Norway, Britain and Germany the debate converged on the 'compact city': meet future needs mainly by densifying inside existing urban boundaries. In Denmark, the Netherlands and partly Sweden, the focus was on local cycles of water, sewage and food, which leads to the 'green city' with a more open, mosaic-like structure and growth by spatial extension. Some went further and rejected cities altogether in favour of rural eco-villages (pp. 507–508).",
     "Næss weighs these against his five criteria and comes down clearly on the side of concentrated, land-saving structures. Dense patterns shorten distances and support public transport and cycling; Oslo data show inner-city residents travel much less by car than outer-area residents even after controlling for socio-economic differences (Figure 1, p. 508); apartments and row houses need less heating per square metre than detached houses (p. 508); and densification converts less nature and farmland (p. 509). Building more roads to relieve congestion simply attracts more car commuting (p. 508).",
     "This is not a plea for a solid 'core city' without green wedges: Frey's figure of about 60 persons per hectare at district level is enough to support local services within walking distance, and at the metropolitan scale some green structure is needed (p. 510). The eco-village model he dismisses as unrealistic: what would happen to French ecosystems 'if all Parisians moved out to settle in dispersed eco-villages?' (p. 511)."

    "h": "Why efficiency and re-use are not enough",
    "p": [
     "Section 5 is the most radical part and the reason the paper is still cited. Suppose planners do everything right: no more sprawl, no more road capacity, most construction inside existing boundaries near the centre. The question remains whether that is sufficient 'as long as the building stock continues to grow' (p. 511). Næss's answer is no. Even if each new building is greener, the total load of the stock rises as it grows: more volume to heat, more materials. New green buildings are only favourable in a relative sense (compared with worse options), unless they replace worse ones (p. 511).",
     "He then gives the numbers. Floor area per person has grown steadily since the Second World War, mainly because dwellings got larger while households got smaller. He asks whether it can be ethically defended to keep raising this level when, for the planet's sake, we must hope poor countries never reach it.",
     "His proposal is 'selective standard improvement' under a national ceiling on resource use (p. 512). Some new dwellings are still needed for population growth, smaller households and people in substandard housing. But then more floor space for people who already live spaciously competes directly with housing for those who lack it. The old route of raising low-income standards by letting the affluent move up and freeing cheaper homes (filtering) no longer works in a resource-limited world."

    "h": "Which planning procedures can deliver this?",
    "p": [
     "Section 6 turns to procedural theory. He first repeats that the few authors who discuss planning procedures and sustainability rarely say what sustainable planning should achieve, and often assume that sustainability goals fit local residents' lifestyles and views, which in affluent societies will hardly be the case (p. 512). Næss's starting point is that in affluent societies sustainability requires cutting activities that make individual life more convenient, enjoyable or prestigious (p. 513), so goals will clash with prevailing lifestyles. He then examines the main planning models in turn.",
     "Unbridled markets cannot do the job, because they do not deal with externalities (costs shifted onto others or the environment, such as pollution) or with fair distribution (p. 513). Incrementalism (Lindblom's 'muddling through', choosing options close to current practice) is also insufficient: if current practice is unsustainable, none of the small steps will change course, radical alternatives never get discussed, and trial-and-error is dangerous when interventions in nature are irreversible (p. 513). Planning must therefore be goal-oriented. But the rational-comprehensive model, which is best at linking means to explicit goals, has its own problems: it shares modernism's technological optimism, neglects lay knowledge, leans on cost-benefit analysis that ignores what cannot be priced, and its utilitarian logic risks 'majority tyranny' over minorities (pp. 513–514).",
     "Communicative planning (Healey's collaborative planning, Innes's consensus-building) gets the most attention. Its belief that dialogue can turn conflicts into win-win situations and that tasks are 'discovered' in communication (Healey, quoted p. 514) is a poor fit for sustainability, because 'a sustainable development is to a high extent a matter of redistributing consumption levels from us who live here and now to those who live there and then' (p. 515). A green, globally fair land use will not emerge spontaneously from the grassroots of rich countries. Local communities also face a 'prisoner's dilemma': why cut emissions if neighbours do not? That calls for coordination at higher scales, so local planning should work within frames set to include non-local consequences (p. 515)."

    "h": "Knowledge, power and the limits of consensus",
    "p": [
     "Næss adds two further objections to consensus-based planning. The first is about knowledge. Communicative theory tends to treat valid knowledge as whatever participants agree on. For ecological facts that fails: 'If the members of a local planning committee agree on the statement that car traffic does not represent any environmental problem, this conclusion will not for that reason be true!' (p. 516). And when planners step back from goals and expert knowledge, the vacuum is not filled by residents' everyday knowledge; it is filled by the experts of banks, developers and road builders, so expertise ends up serving only those who can pay for it (p. 516). He wants expert and lay knowledge combined, across natural, technical, social and human sciences.",
     "The second objection is about power. Current unsustainable development is no accident: some actors want it because it produces profit and growth. A model that does not accept the right of a majority to decide against a minority's interests is poorly suited to confront those actors (p. 517), especially since keeping consumption within limits would sharpen class conflicts over distribution. Here Næss joins the critics (Flyvbjerg, Tewdwr-Jones and Allmendinger) who call collaborative planning naive about power. Note the tension with his earlier worry about 'majority tyranny': he wants majorities able to overrule privileged interests, but minorities' vital interests protected."

    "h": "What planners can do now",
    "p": [
     "Section 7 is practical. Næss admits there is little political support for ambitious sustainability policy in any European country, and that outside pressure (for example a tougher Kyoto follow-up) might help (p. 517). Still, planners can act: assess the consequences of alternatives against sustainability criteria, especially energy and biodiversity, and share that with politicians and the public; draw up sustainable alternatives themselves and start a debate about them; and use their municipality's official sustainability goals or national goals to legitimise this (p. 517).",
     "Because planners who expose the unsustainable plans of powerful groups risk being called disloyal or even fired, he proposes legislation requiring impact analysis and public monitoring of strategic plans against sustainability indicators, for more transparency and accountability (pp. 517–518). He rejects the opposition 'participatory democracy good, representative democracy bad': deliberation (amalgamating arguments) and voting (aggregating preferences) should be combined across arenas, from neighbourhood meetings to the council, sometimes with a referendum (p. 518). Following Forester, planners should counter 'distorted communication' and 'contain the strong and empower the weak', or participation brings 'more power to the powerful' (p. 518)."

    "h": "Conclusions: scenarios and alliance-building",
    "p": [
     "The final section offers two tools. Scenarios can make abstract, long-term and global issues tangible by contrasting principally different strategies; the Norwegian NAMIT project's 'trend' versus 'environmental' scenarios raised awareness among planners and probably contributed to less sprawling development in Norway in the 1990s (p. 519). Developed in interdisciplinary groups, scenarios make planning a learning process and can reveal both shared interests and fundamental antagonisms.",
     "Alliance-building is the second and main tool. Oil companies will not join a consensus to restrict cars, and business coalitions with leading politicians often block environmental policy (Flyvbjerg's Aalborg study; Logan and Molotch's growth machine) (p. 519). Growth coalitions already use alliance-building; Næss wants planners to do the same for sustainability. 'In my opinion, aiming to achieve consensus among all stakeholders is futile' (p. 520). Consensus is possible, and useful, within groups that share basic values and have no stake in the most harmful sectors, making them stronger against their opponents. His hope is modest: open, well-informed processes might build strategies for ecological sustainability and social justice with enough support to withstand those who profit from the status quo."
